# On Technological Determinism — Fast Read (all main points)

**Author:** Allan Dafoe | **Science, Technology, & Human Values** 40(6): 1047–76, 2015 (online Apr 14, 2015)
**What this is:** An STS theory article that reclaims “technological determinism” from its use as a critic’s insult, maps the family of claims, names the scale-of-analysis scope condition, and offers a microfoundation: military–economic adaptationism, a special case of sociotechnical selectionism.

**Core claim / thesis.** Technological determinism is a valuable perspective: claims that put more weight on technology’s autonomy and social-shaping power. Constructivist and determinist insights can both be valid under different scope conditions—especially scale of analysis. The mechanism is military–economic competition, which constrains sociotechnical evolution onto deterministic paths. Technology opens a door; competition drags us through.

---

## Who—if anyone—controls technological change?

- Central question: how much control do we have over tools, production systems, social relations, worldview? How much is thrust on us by elites, path-dependent past choices, or an internal technological logic?
- Pre-1980s: many scholars (Winner 1977) took seriously that technological change could be an out-of-control, history-shaping process—autonomous, internally logical, unintended social effects, especially over long spans.
- Since then, sociologists/historians of technology dismiss this as “technological determinism.” They prefer constructivism: narrative, context, agency, interpretive flexibility, contingency.
- Constructivism has been productive: social groups, historical context, and varying meanings of a technology matter. It killed *simplistic* determinisms.

**STS then overshot.** It largely rejected the questions at the core of determinism.

- Kline (2001): historians and sociologists have “discredited” TD; it is now “a critic’s term and a term of abuse.”
- Lynch (2008): TD reduced to “a straw position.”
- Dafoe’s count: 60 top Google Scholar hits for “technological determinism” in *STHV*, *Social Studies of Science*, and *Technology and Culture*; of 25 papers that judged TD, **76% were critical** (data: http://dx.doi.org/10.7910/DVN/28473).
- Most STS still grants an “influence of technology upon social relations” (Mackenzie and Wajcman 1999), but neglects effects and autonomy.
- Underexplored: political effects of technology; inertia of systems; trends and internal logic; historical transformations from key innovations. (Note 1: some neighboring-discipline work does this—Rosenkopf & Tushman, Schroeder, Radder, Feenberg, Leonardi & Barley.)
- Leonardi and Barley (2010): the field swung so far from TD that it neglected “materiality and power.”
- Other social sciences, business, and the military still take deterministic questions seriously. STS no longer engages one of its founding debates.
- Wyatt (2008) and Söderberg (2013) “take TD more seriously” only as *beliefs to explain*, not as theories that can explain.

**Reclaim the term.** Use “technological determinism” for work closer to the determinist side of a continuum (Smith and Marx 1994), not as an insult.

**Figure 1 continuum:** Radical Social Constructivism → (Mild) Social Constructivism → (Soft) Technological Determinism → Hard Technological Determinism.

- Extremes (hard TD; radical constructivism) are generally implausible.
- Wrong question: is TD right or wrong?
- Right questions: **to what extent, in what ways, and under what scope conditions** are particular technologies more autonomous and more powerful in shaping society? Complement: when can particular groups shape sociotechnical systems?

**Roadmap (four contributions):**
1. Clarify debates via the meaning of *technology*.
2. Parse the family of TD claims.
3. Scope conditions—especially scale of analysis. Macro TD and micro constructivism need not cancel each other.
4. Missing microfoundation: **military–economic adaptationism**. At the micro level, groups can have extreme flexibility; at the macro level, intense military/economic competition can make technology evolve *as if* by an inner logic that determines society.

## What is technology?

Confusion about TD is partly confusion about the word.

- *Technology* can mean vast sociotechnical systems (the Internet) *or* specific artifacts, standards, routines, beliefs (computers, IP, email habits, trust in online info).
- Leo Marx (1997): conflating artifact and system induces bad deterministic thinking; the abstract term is “almost completely vacuous.” Prefer precise terms when possible.

**Dafoe’s glossary:**
- **Artifact** — objects intended for a function (machines, devices, tools).
- **Technique** — “softer” functional configurations (habits of mind, analytical methods, routines).
- **Institutions** — organizational hierarchies, legal codes, incentive structures.
- **Sociotechnical systems** — vast functional configurations of all of the above.

He still keeps *technology*. Like its precursors (*machine, invention, improvement, mechanic/useful arts*), it is rooted in the machine metaphor and the application of science to commercial and military aims. It highlights **functionality**.

**Definitions he keeps:**
- “a manner of accomplishing a task” (Merriam-Webster 2005)
- “configurations that work” (Rip and Kemp 1998)

**Defining characteristic = functionality, not materiality.** Technology (1) names entities that are or were functional, and (2) emphasizes their functional dimension.

**Payoff for the debate:** critics often treat TD as *artifactual* determinism. Some of it is. The core literature is **functional determinism**: history often seems to follow inexorable functional logics that drive and are driven by technological change.

## Technological determinism (definition + typology)

**Critic’s extreme (Kline 2001):** (1) technology develops by an “internal logic independent of social influence”; (2) “technological change determines social change in a prescribed manner.”

**Bimber’s extreme (1994):** history “determined by laws … rather than by human will,” and those laws require physical artifacts. That definition erases almost all work that takes technology’s effects seriously.

**Dafoe’s moderate definition:** approaches that emphasize
1. **autonomy of technological change**
2. **technological shaping of society**

Harder = more autonomy and power for technology. Softer = more social control and context. Umbrella for respectable work across disciplines.

**Family of claims (theories italicized):**
1. Functional entities (artifacts, techniques, institutions, systems) affect the world independent of human choice → ***technical determinism***.
2. Broad sequence and tempo of scientific/technological advance (***technological trends***) that seems to follow an internal logic → change looks autonomous.
3. People are insufficiently conscious of technological choices (***technological somnambulism***) or have been co-opted (***the magnificent bribe***), so the social order becomes more machine-like over time.

---

## Technical determinism

**Artifactual determinism** (the usual straw): artifacts shape social relations.

**Technical determinism** (broader): artifacts, routines, and the technical dimensions of institutions/systems exert effects. Harder variants: more extensive and powerful.

**Technological politics** (Winner 1980, 1986) — most room for agency. Intentions inscribed in technologies then influence others. Tech decisions “are similar to legislative acts or political foundings” that set public order for generations. Examples: fences, speed bumps, bulletproof glass, surveillance, encryption, linear Parisian boulevards for riot suppression (Lay 1992). Prisons, schools, art studios use tech to evoke compliance or creativity. Latour (1992): tech is the “missing mass” of sociology. Feenberg (2010): tech’s “bias” = “material validation of the social order.” Conversation with STS still thin (Hamlett 2003; Wachelder 2003).

**Technological momentum** (Hughes 1983) and **technological frames** (Bijker 1995) — more deterministic than politics because constraints need not be designed by anyone. Mature systems gain inertia via sunk costs: assets bought, standards set, infrastructure built, employees trained, interactions routinized, interests entrenched. Bijker: groups cannot “fantasize whatever they want”; frames “constrain freedom of choice in designing new technologies.”

**Unintended consequences as the bridge to hard TD.** Almost no one denies technology can influence social relations. That premise implies harder determinism whenever consequences are unforeseen—lack of foresight/concern, or sheer complexity.

Examples:
- Machine gun + barbed wire → a form of war abhorred even by the generals who continued it (Ellis 1975).
- Skolt Lapps: a few snowmobiles in herding undermined egalitarian culture (Pelto 1973).
- US National Security Council, *Global Trends—2015*: people will lack full awareness of wider economic, environmental, cultural, legal, and moral impact.
- Winner (1977): if change is too fast/extensive, societies may go “adrift in a vast sea of ‘unintended consequences.’”

**Limit case:** if technical effects are powerful and consequences unforeseeable, the mainstream STS premise *implies* hard TD: technology evolves as if autonomous and society-determining.

**Therefore:** the gap between mainstream STS and hard determinism is **degree**, not kind—how strongly tech shapes relations, how foreseeable the consequences. Merit of hard TD is **empirical and context-specific**, not something to assume, deduce, or casually generalize.

## Trends in technological change

Over long time: artifact pool grows in diversity and number; later innovations exceed earlier ones in complexity, power, utility.

**“Trends in the maximum”** (easiest to define): rising maxima of transport/communication speed, weapon lethality, material durability, engine efficiency, labor productivity, information storage/reproduction, building height, etc. Other trend types could be operationalized.

- Strayer (1955): in medieval Western Europe, “if there is steady progress anywhere, it is in the field of technology.”
- Anthropologists/archaeologists: direction toward greater social complexity. Wright (2000): “the deeper you dig, the simpler the society.”
- Need not be monotonic at every scale to count.
- Some follow from cumulative innovation (Heilbroner 1967): nuclear power after simpler power, not before.

**Internal-logic trends.** Ceruzzi (2005) on computing: “an internal logic is at work.” Chip density’s exponential slope (Moore’s Law) barely wavered for 40 years. Belief in Moore’s Law is “an indication of the reality of technological determinism. Computing power must increase because it can.” Historians should “step back from a social constructionist view”; in some cases “raw technological determinism is at work.”

**Rationalization / la technique.**
- Weber: rational calculation reduces the worker to a cog who only wants to become a bigger cog.
- Ellul (1962): *la technique* is “artificial, autonomous, self-determining, and independent of all human intervention.”
- Mumford (1966): “man will become a passive, purposeless, machine conditioned animal.”
- Mechanism 1: ***technological somnambulism*** (Winner 1977) — insufficient awareness of choices.
- Mechanism 2: ***the magnificent bribe*** (Mumford 1964) — system co-opts people with material incentive to become a “somewhat bigger cog.” Each member may claim “every material advantage, every intellectual and emotional stimulus” in exchange for surrender to “authoritarian technics.” Winner: every group with power is on the technostructure’s payroll.

**Example — *Technology Review*, 1997–98.** Declining ad revenue; Fortune ad director hired; “nothing will be left of the old magazine except the name” (Warsh 1998). Staff fired. From policy-reflective to “cheerleading for innovation” (former editor Marcus). Profit remade the conversation. How many other public conversations are similarly bought?

**Alternative:** trends as *chosen*. Leo Marx (1987) “technocratic concept of progress”: scientific/tech/economic progress thought to improve “all the conditions of life.” If people believe that, trends may be social choice, not determinism. Counter: plenty of historical trends ran *against* elite will (Meiji, below). Development may only temporarily match most people’s interests. IT and automation are plausibly eroding most Americans’ income. (Note 2: IGM economists 2014 — 33% agree vs 20% disagree that IT/automation are the central reason median US wages stagnated.)

**Trend summary:** some from prerequisites; some so rhythmic they look internally logical; rationalization from unconsciousness, co-optation, or perceived benefit.

## Scope conditions and levels of analysis

Most “deterministic” trends are **long**: 5 decades of transistor density; 2 centuries since the Industrial Revolution; 2 millennia of civilizational size, complexity, energy intensity.

**Misa (1988):** TD scholarship uses larger time/space scales; constructivist scholarship uses smaller ones. Across *and* within disciplines: affirmer = macro; denier = micro.

**Micro-supremacy response (wrong):** detailed small-scale work is more valid, so it refutes macro TD.
- Misa (1998): “the Machine as causal force vanishes” under detailed analysis; from the shop floor the Machine is “an irrelevant abstraction”; history is individuals/classes in conflict or accommodation; “a row of machine tools is not itself a compelling historical agent.”
- Williams (2002): to a historian, TD is “self-evidently untrue: human beings construct machines, not the reverse.”
- Implicit anytime micro evidence is used to kill macro findings.

**Dafoe’s reply:** different processes can operate at different scales (Leonardi and Barley 2010). Complex systems have **emergent** macro patterns invisible at micro (Byrne 1998).

**Traffic analogy:** drivers have intelligence, idiosyncrasy, free will; traffic still follows simple, counterintuitive, unintended logic. Slowing traffic can raise throughput (Resnick 1996). Jams emerge from density, no accident required (Nagel and Paczuski 1995).

**Don’t reject macro because you lack a micro story.** Analogies: don’t reject Boyle’s Law because molecules are chaotic; don’t reject moon–tide correlation because you lack Newton.

**Scope condition:** limited to their empirical domains, constructivist *and* determinist claims can both be valid. Micro: socially created, hacked, interpreted. Macro: trends, internal logic, history shaped in ways no one foresaw.

## The missing mechanism: military–economic competition

Macro observations get stronger if linked to a microfoundation.

**Military–economic adaptationism** can account for most determinist claims.

**Statement:** (C1) when economic and/or military competition is sufficiently intense and prolonged, and (C2) technology enables new sociotechnical configurations, (C3) some of which confer economic/military advantages → sociotechnical systems evolve to become more adapted to success in that competition.

**General theory: sociotechnical selectionism.** Mild constructivism and military–economic adaptationism are special cases, differing on *which* selection process dominates (cognitive/social vs. economic/military).

---

## Sociotechnical selectionism

Competition-as-mechanism is a special case of **selectionism / Universal Darwinism**: variation + differential proliferation. Forms that proliferate (reproduce, survive, grow) because of a trait become more abundant. Interactors (Hull 2001) come to look *as if designed for* the selection environment.

Applies to sociotechnical entities: many hammers, production strategies, militaries; new variants keep appearing. Some types are reproduced, imitated, imported, or scaled; most die out. Pressures: cognitive, social, economic, military, ecological, etc. Long run: hammers more effective, production more productive, militaries more powerful.

- **Military–economic adaptationism** = selectionism that emphasizes economic/military pressures. Those pressures run on longer timescales than cognitive/social ones → why TD claims cluster in long-run studies.
- **Mild constructivism** = selectionism whose environment is mainly cognitive, social, or political. Pinch and Bijker (1987) already said SCOT is “an alternation of variation and selection.” Also Basalla, Vincenti, O’Connell, Ziman (ed.) *Technological Innovation as an Evolutionary Process*.
- Selectionism does **not** imply linear evolution (SCOT: “multidirectional model”), but *can* produce trends (next section).
- Selectionist reasoning also in psychology (Campbell), philosophy (Dennett), political science, economics, archaeology, biology.

---

## Military–economic adaptationism — conditions and implications

Applies when three conditions hold.

**Condition 1 (military–economic competition).** Sufficiently intense and prolonged military and/or economic competition between groups, such that outcomes determine whether groups can maintain and proliferate their ways of life. Intensity varies by region and era; measurable; variation can be exploited (Turchin et al. 2013). This is the binding scope condition.

**Condition 2 (technology enables).** Technology enables new sociotechnical configurations. Mildest possible “effect of technology.” Does *not* assume constraint, valence, or deterministic social effects. Only: some technologies let groups do what they otherwise could not. Almost always true.

**Condition 3 (imperfection).** Newly enabled configurations are *sometimes* superior for the selection environment. Rules out the implausible case that new tech can never help. How often superior configs become feasible per unit time sets the **speed** of adaptation.

**Implication 1 (differential proliferation).** All else equal, groups that adopt economically or militarily advantageous configurations proliferate relative to those that do not.

**Implication 2 (military–economic adaptation).** Sociotechnical systems evolve to be more adapted to the economic and/or military competitive environment.

**Determinists already said this:**
- Ellul (1962): *la technique* proliferates because it is powerful; the choice to adopt or reject is illusory—adopt or be defeated.
- Mumford (1962): societies pursued technical means because of economic and military competition.
- Mokyr (1990): Europe’s “struggle for survival” guaranteed rulers had to accommodate economically beneficial advances. Without competition, progress “would probably have been willfully stopped long ago.” “Forces opposing technological progress have been stronger than those striving for changes.” But “as long as some societies remain creative, others will eventually be dragged along.” Proximally stronger anti-tech forces lose to groups that became more competitive via their attitude toward technology.
- Slogan: so long as some communities pursue profitable or powerful technologies, **all societies will eventually be “dragged along.”**

**Where else this mechanism lives:** economics of innovation; evolutionary economics; economic history; welfare theorems; path dependence / increasing returns (Arthur); rational-actor assumption in competitive markets; IR structural realism (Waltz: competition constrains statesmen to pursue military–economic power regardless of ultimate goals); origins of the state/nation-state (Carneiro, Tilly, Spruyt, Thompson, Turchin); even altruism-promoting norms/institutions/preferences (Bowles and Gintis). History, sociology, anthropology, archaeology (McNeill, Sanderson, Diamond, Carneiro, Wilson, Richerson and Boyd).

**Power critique of constructivism extended.** Russell; Klein and Kleinman: some constructivism ignores social structure / asymmetric power. Adaptationism adds: when competition is severe, **power goes to those who best adapt** to military–economic pressures.

---

## Implication 3 — trends

**Implication 3 (trends).** If the competitive environment keeps selecting for more of a trait (energy intensiveness, functional differentiation, processor speed, explosive power), sociotechnical evolution will show trends in that trait.

Determinists see linear evolution; constructivists see multidirectional. Selectionism: directionality depends on the selection process over time.

**Even under military–economic competition, trends are not automatic.** Broad historical trend toward larger polities and more functional differentiation, but **iron weaponry, 1000–1200 BC**, made flatter structures more competitive and *reduced* (for a time) polity size and differentiation (McNeill 1984).

Trends arise if the *same* trait is selected over time. Whether that happens in sociotechnical evolution (or in biology—McShea 1998) is an open question.

**Better questions:** Which selection processes produce trends? Did historical periods have those processes? Do we observe the trends? Where are current processes taking us?

## Vicarious selection

Vincenti (1990) and others: a life-form **anticipates** the external selection environment and adapts in advance. Speeds adaptation; **hides the ultimate cause** from the analyst.

**Aeronautics example:** don’t build every wing and crash it. Sequence: cognitive model → group’s idea of good design → wind tunnel. Still must pass the *external* test: move a plane profitably or militarily.

**Payoff:** explaining tech change by decisions, beliefs, values, and ideologies alone misses prior structural causes. Obsession with power and profit has proximal causes in culture/ideology/institutions/preferences; ultimate driver may be survival and proliferation of states and firms.

Given competition and time, non-adapters are **defeated, absorbed, or they imitate**. Imitation as proliferation: DiMaggio and Powell; Rogers; Lynn; Goldman and Eliason. Micro imitation looks willful, psychological, or cultural; don’t underestimate competitive pressure underneath.

**Britain’s electric systems (Hughes).** Pre-WWI British systems smaller than US/German because “the British placed a high value on the power of local government.” WWI efficiency imperative: networked and enlarged, contrary to “prevailing British political values.” Engineers committed to smallness still “acknowledged the primacy of output when personal and national survival seemed to depend on it.” Energy production became larger, more centralized, more complex and differentiated *despite* political views.

> The British chose to have a larger power system in the trivial sense that a victim chooses to give up his wallet at gunpoint.

**Tokugawa Japan / Meiji (best case of a society controlling its tech course—then losing that control).**
- 16th c.: some of the world’s most sophisticated firearms (Perrin 1979). Firearms helped unify Japan by favoring larger warlords (Chase 2003; Brown 1948).
- After unification, internal/external military competition subsided → elites recaptured destiny: firearms centralized then largely eliminated; foreign interchange strictly controlled; samurai order for 200+ years.
- 1853: Commodore Perry’s four “black ships of evil” (Baruma 2004) violate seclusion—smoke, moving upwind without sails. Implicit threat of bombardment and famine blockade (Samson 1963). Concessions. China as colonial warning. Debate, then civil war. New leaders systematically imitate foreign institutions/tech that seemed to confer military power.
- Within decades: radical Westernizing transformation, contrary to pre-1853 elite values. Neighbors colonized; Japan modernized “on its own terms” (Totman; Sims)—but only in a very limited sense.
- Accurate description: Japan **adapted to military pressure**.

> Stark military–economic competition reduces our choice set to two options: **adapt or be dominated.**

## Limits and issues

- Military–economic competition is **not necessary** for TD. Effects, inertia, and unintended consequences can generate some TD features on their own. Adaptationism is a mechanism for many of the *harder* claims.
- **Not a complete theory.** Only for domains where the conditions hold—especially C1. Modern military competition is much less severe than 100 or 1,000 years ago (Mueller 2007; Pinker 2011). Countries can worry less about conquest and more about consumption. Expect **military** adaptationism to be *less* useful for contemporary global change.

**Kinds of military–economic competition** (different implications):
- Interstate military
- Interstate economic
- Interfirm transnational economic
- Within-state economic

**More deterministic** = rules more given by **nature**, not preferences/norms/culture/law. Most deterministic: military competition between groups that do *not* share culture or institutions. Least: economic competition inside a well-regulated economy (fitness landscape set by norms, culture, taste, law → more socially controllable). Firms still adapt under severe economic competition, but if society controls the *rules*, it can control the adaptation beneath them.

Military competition can also be socially regulated: nuclear taboo; laws of war (Morrow 2014). Norms likelier when belligerents share a culture and the gain from violating them is not too large. Most of history: military competition always present (Gat 2006); restraints periodically overrun in intense wars.

> The more we value social control of history, the more we should understand different kinds of military and economic competition **and regulate them accordingly.**

**Other selection processes** (cognitive, social, ecological) can run at once, at different levels, in different directions, on different timescales, interacting. Example needing both constructivist and economic-adaptationist theory: advertising—firms compete to optimize the technology of socially constructing marketable desires. Agents can also change the rules (lobbying; victors remaking regional order). Biology’s name: **niche construction**. “Selection pressures” = whatever determines differential proliferation; adaptiveness is emergent.

**Just-so-story risk.** If the selection process is unclear and “adaptiveness” is not self-evident, adaptationism becomes too flexible / unfalsifiable. Flexibility ≠ false, but needs discipline:
1. Independent evidence of the selection process (process-trace that the trait actually helped proliferation).
2. Predictions blind to the outcome, ideally out of sample.
3. State all testable implications and scope conditions.

**Ecological selection.** Differential proliferation by how well groups handle toxins, pathogens, extreme climate / climate change, sustainable resource access. Maladapted customs/institutions perish—abandoned or the groups perish (Diamond 2005). Ecological collapse can “masquerade as military defeats.”

**Relative vs. absolute performance.**
- Ecological: A slightly fitter than B → A proliferates *slightly* faster.
- Economic: small efficiency edge often → much faster proliferation. In a perfectly competitive market, the smallest efficiency difference decides who lives.
- Military: slight absolute advantage can yield huge contest advantage.
- Competing with others, not just “against nature”: **not enough to do well; must continually do better.**

Ecological selection is slower. A way of life can be adapted to social/economic/military pressures and still be **maladapted ecologically**. Open question: how modern civilization fares under climate change.

## Conclusion — who controls technological change?

**It depends.** Agency depends on the character and intensity of selection and the character of variation. Neither radical constructivism nor hard TD is a satisfactory general account.

- **Smaller scales / weak competition:** constructivist insights most valid—tremendous interpretive flexibility and choice.
- **Larger scales / strong military–economic competition:** determinist claims more valid—evolution of forms directed; destiny tightly constrained.

Do not judge theories by how much agency they *allow*. Judge how well they explain. Assuming people “have choice” and assuming they have none are **equally disempowering**. Ask how much and what kinds of agency, in which circumstances, and why.

- Hard TD: discourages political action (change is inevitable).
- Radical constructivism: handicaps action by ignoring competitive forces.

**Research agenda if TD is taken seriously:**
- Effects of different kinds of technology?
- Parse/evaluate: technological politics, momentum, internal logic of development, unintended consequences, the magnificent bribe, technological progress, military–economic adaptationism?
- When are these more or less socially controllable?
- How have societies regulated economic competition toward desired goals?
- Can we build global cultures, institutions, and norms that regulate military–economic competition toward desired goals?

**What selectionism does for the field:**
- Illuminates agency via intensity of selection pressures.
- Links levels of analysis.
- Gives technology studies a general framework across the social sciences.

**Questions for constructivists whose claims generalize beyond the study’s spatiotemporal scope:**
1. Are there longer-run selection processes that constrain agents’ ability to socially construct technologies?
2. How did groups come by their beliefs and preferences—could those be adaptations to perceived competitive pressures?

Also: how groups harness/guide military–economic competition, and why that succeeds or fails. Vicarious selection: what experiences persuade a group it must adapt its technologies and social order? How do people learn and internalize external pressures?

**Closing microfoundation recap.** Humans generate new ways of living. Technology enables new sociotechnical forms. Survival/proliferation depends on fit to selection pressures. Long-run military–economic competition has selected for militarily powerful, economically productive forms—hence trends toward large, complex, energy-intensive, technical, functionally differentiated, rationalistic systems. Selection for power and productivity may explain many macro-patterns determinists observed.

Lynn White (stirrup → feudalism): technology “merely opens a door; it does not compel one to enter.”

> He was right. Technology opens the door. It is military–economic competition that drags us through.

- Wrong question: right or wrong? Right questions: extent, ways, scope conditions.
- Defining mark of technology is **functionality**, not materiality. Core TD is **functional** determinism, not just artifactual.
- Mainstream STS already implies hard TD once effects are strong and consequences unforeseeable—difference of degree, empirical and context-specific.
