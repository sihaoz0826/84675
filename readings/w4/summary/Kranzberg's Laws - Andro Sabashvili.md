# Kranzberg’s Laws — Fast Read (all main points)

**Author:** Andro Sabashvili | **Date:** April 10, 2024
**What this is:** A short summary of Melvin Kranzberg’s 1986 *Technology and Culture* essay *Technology and History: Kranzberg’s Laws*. Each law is stated, given a one-line gloss, then backed with Kranzberg’s own explanation.

**Core claim:** Six laws for thinking about technology and history. Technology is not morally simple; inventions create new needs; technologies come as systems; policy is driven by nontechnical factors; the history of technology is the most relevant history; and technology is a human activity that remakes humans.

---

## First Law

**Technology is neither good nor bad; nor is it neutral.**

**Gloss:** Everything is relative. Technology has complex, unpredictable impacts on the social ecology. Costs and benefits must be evaluated in different contexts and time frames.

- Tech interacts with the social ecology: effects go far beyond the immediate purposes of the devices/practices.
- The same technology can have quite different results in different contexts or circumstances.
- Many tech-related problems come from unforeseen consequences when apparently benign technologies are used at massive scale.
- Unforeseen **dis-benefits** can come from presumably beneficent technologies.
- Example: nuclear technology → prospect of unlimited energy **and** possibility of worldwide destruction.
- Always compare: short-term vs long-term; utopian hopes vs spotted actuality; what-might-have-been vs what happened; trade-offs among goods and possible bads.
- Can only do this by seeing how tech interacts with different values, institutions, and the entire **sociocultural milieu**.

---

## Second Law

**Invention is the mother of necessity.**

**Gloss:** Technical innovations often need further inventions to be fully effective.

- Every technical innovation seems to require additional technical advances to become fully effective.
- Example: Bell’s telephone spawned further tech — Edison’s carbon-granule microphone, central-switching mechanisms.

---

## Third Law

**Technology comes in packages, big and small.**

**Gloss:** Complex mechanisms are systems of interconnected components. Change one part and the others must adjust. Do not analyze components separately; study how they interact.

- Today’s complex mechanisms usually involve several processes and components.
- Example: **radar** — specialized materials, power sources, devices to send waves of the right frequency, detect the bounce, interpret them, and put results on a screen.
- In *Networks of Power*, what Kranzberg calls **packages** Thomas Hughes more precisely calls **systems**: coherent structures of interacting, interconnected components.
- When one component changes, other parts must transform so the system can keep functioning.
- Parts cannot be viewed in isolation; study them through their interrelations.

---

## Fourth Law

**Although technology might be a prime element in many public issues, nontechnical factors take precedence in technology-policy decisions.**

**Gloss:** Technology-policy decisions depend more on nontechnical factors (social and political factors, plus the public’s perception of risk) than on technology. **No risk is the highest risk of all.**

- Hughes, *Networks of Power*: nontechnical factors shaped electrical-network growth — Chicago vs Berlin vs London.
  - **Chicago:** private enterprise (Samuel Insull) followed the most efficient technology, seeking economies of scale.
  - **Berlin and London:** municipal governments cared more about their own authority than technical efficiency; political infighting; they lagged on the most economical power networks.
- **Nuclear power** is especially politicized.
  - Industry partly to blame for technological deficiencies.
  - Public presumption of risk after **Three Mile Island** and **Chernobyl** changed the future of what was once seen as a safe, inexhaustible power source.
  - Public fears catastrophic consequences from nuclear generators.
  - Historical claim: no one killed by commercial nuclear power accidents in this country.
  - Contrast: **50,000 Americans killed each year by automobiles**.
- Gap is partly **perception of risk**, not actual risk. People seek a **zero-risk society**.
- Aaron Wildavsky: **“No risk is the highest risk of all.”**

---

## Fifth Law

**All history is relevant, but the history of technology is the most relevant.**

**Gloss:** History is a key to understanding the future, but it is often taught without technology — even though technology has shaped daily life, the arts, and humanistic endeavors throughout history.

- History is a fundamental liberal art and essential as a key to the future.
- Many students do not see history’s relevance to present or future — because most history, as taught, **ignores the technological element**.
- **Annales** school social historians: technology set the patterns of daily life for the vast majority of people throughout history.
- History of the arts is most guilty of neglecting technology — they treat tech as mere mechanical devices and miss both direct influence on the arts and indirect influence on humanistic endeavors.
- Art/Renaissance historians should see that **Leonardo** and **Michelangelo** were also great engineers.
- That relationship continues: **David Billington** on structural design and art.

---

## Sixth Law

**Technology is a very human activity - and so is the history of technology.**

**Gloss (italic on the page):** *Technology is created by humans and humans are changed by technology.* Technology is both a product and a factor of human development; mutual dependence and influence.

- Kreisler story: a woman says, “Maestro, your violin makes such beautiful music.” He holds it to his ear: “I don’t hear any music coming our of it.”
- Hardware/violin is useless without the human; Kreisler cannot make music without the instrument.
- History of technology = story of **man and tool - hand and mind - working together**.
- Faulty hardware or deficient software → discordant sounds.
- When man and machine work together, they can make some beautiful music.
