# Technological Determinism Is Dead; Long Live Technological Determinism — Fast Read (all main points)

**Author:** Sally Wyatt | **In:** *The Handbook of Science and Technology Studies*, 3rd ed., eds. Hackett, Amsterdamska, Lynch, Wajcman (MIT Press, 2008), 165–180.

**What this is:** An STS handbook chapter. Wyatt argues technological determinism is *not* dead. It still organizes how actors, analysts, policy makers, and ordinary users talk and act. STS must stop sneering at it, split it into types, and treat actors’ TD theories symmetrically with analysts’ theories.

**Core claim / thesis.** We cannot ignore technological determinism and hope it disappears. Take it seriously: disentangle the types and the work they do; recognize the technological determinists *within ourselves*. Four types: **justificatory, descriptive, methodological, normative**.

## “Technological determinism is dead, or is it?”

TD **persists** in five places (examples later):
1. Actions and justifications of actors
2. Analysts making sense of tech in social settings
3. Theoretical/abstract accounts of technical vs social
4. Policy makers/politicians defending new technologies
5. Everyday reactions to new machines and new ways of doing things

**Naming habit (common sense before it had a label)**
- Arendt: tools/instruments are so worldly we classify whole civilizations by them.
- Ages: stone, iron, steam, computer.
- Nations: Holland–windmills, US–cars, Japan–microelectronics.
- Heilbroner & Edgerton: *availability of different machines* defines what it is like to live in a place and time.
- Mumford: anthropology/archaeology started the habit — nonliterate societies, artifacts as sole record → “Beaker Men,” “Double Axe Men,” “Glazed Pottery Men”; artifacts in a progressive series look like self-propelling tech advance needing no culture. No excuse for this reductionism in contemporary societies, but museums, schoolbooks, newspapers, TV/radio still do it. Broers 2005 Reith Lectures: “Technology will determine the future of the human race” / series title “The Triumph of Technology.” Still hard to say which 20th-century tech will name the century.

## Definition of technological determinism (keep both parts)

Critics named this habit “technological determinism.” **Two parts, both necessary** (she follows MacKenzie & Wajcman, not Misa):

1. Tech develops **outside society**, independently of social/economic/political forces. Inventors, engineers, designers follow an **internal technical logic**.
2. **More crucial:** technological change **causes or determines** social change.

**Misa (1988):** treats these as *two different versions*. That lets “scourges of TD” net Winner and Ellul as determinists because they point to capitalist rationality — she says that is **materialism/realism**, not TD; if anything, **economic determinism**.

**Feenberg (note 4), same two premises:** “unilinear progress” + “determination by the base.”

STS for ~25 years mostly attacked **part 1** via rich historical/ethnographic studies (Latour & Woolgar; Traweek; *SSS*; *ST&HV*).

**Progress equation:** TD is imbued with **technological progress = social progress**.
- Lenin: “Communism is Soviet power plus the electrification of the whole country.”
- Politicians of all stripes. Bush: missile defense; 2006 SOTU — tech as solution to the US energy crisis.
- **Pessimistic TD:** Ellul, Marcuse, Frankfurt School.

**Historical folk model:** each generation produces a few inventors; failures → dust heap; successes integrate and transform society. Breakthrough → social consequences.

**Why it endures**
- Simplicity.
- Matches most people’s experience: everyday tech is mysterious in origin and design; we adapt and hope it keeps working as sold. So TD remains the **“common sense” explanation**.

**Political problem:** leaves no space for human choice/intervention; **absolves us of responsibility** for technologies we make and use. Serves developers (consumer products *or* power stations). Lets us deny responsibility and ridicule people who challenge the pace/direction of change.

**Chapter’s job:** cannot ignore TD hoping the world will embrace “indeterminancy” and complexity. STS cannot just despair and do subtler analyses. Must take TD seriously, disentangle types, clarify purposes in specific circumstances — and **recognize the technological determinists within ourselves**.

## A brief and symmetrical detour (table 7.1)

**Table 7.1 — Extending the Principle of Symmetry** (first three columns from Bijker 1995: 275)

| | Bloor on science | Pinch & Bijker on technology | Callon on socio-technology | Wyatt on method in STS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Impartiality | statement true or false | machine success or failure | actor human or nonhuman | actor identified by other actors *or* the analyst |
| Symmetry | explaining truth and falsity | explaining success and failure | explaining social world *and* technical world | using concepts from analysts *and* actors |
| Result, not cause | what we take to be nature is the result, not the cause, of a statement becoming a true fact | “Working” is the result, not the cause, of a machine becoming a successful artifact | “technical”/“social” distinction is the result, not the cause, of stabilizing socio-technical ensembles | “Success” is the result, not the cause, of a machine becoming a working artifact |

Two points:

**1. Success/failure and working/nonworking (classical Bloor → Pinch & Bijker → Callon → Wyatt)**
- Bloor: true *and* false knowledge claims need the same sociological explanation. Nature is the *result* of a claim being accepted as true, not the cause.
- For technology: successful and failed machines need the same *social* terms.
- Pinch & Bijker: “working is the result and not the cause of a machine becoming a successful artifact.” For unequivocally successful systems this is **tautological**. Better tests: **ambiguous** systems (esp. IT — hard to score success/failure, working/nonworking).
- Wyatt’s ICT-network systems in US/UK central government: they **worked, were not successful, and no longer work**.
- Postmodern reverse (Derrida: signified always already signifier / “X is always already Y”): **“success is the result and not the cause of a machine becoming a working artifact.”** That shows Callon’s point: sociotechnical divide is a *consequence* of stabilization, not a prior cause.
- Pinch & Bijker still presume the divide (success = social; working = technical) and **privilege the social** by putting success before working. Reverse the claim to show **mutual constitution**. Successive symmetry extensions lead back to **classical realism**. “Success” and “working” must be interchangeable. Bottom cells of Pinch & Bijker and Wyatt are **two sides of the same coin**, not alternatives.

**2. Treat actors’ and analysts’ concepts symmetrically** (Wyatt’s middle cell)
- Limits of “follow the actors” (Latour): miss groups invisible to actors (Bijsterveld; Martin & Scott; Russell; Winner 1993). Users often overlooked (Oudshoorn & Pinch, ch. 22).
- Information networks: **at least two user sets** — (a) conventional users (employees using the system to do the job); (b) clients/customers the first group serves, with different interests.
- Distinguish **“real” users** vs **images of users** held by designers/engineers/system builders.
- **“Implicated users”** (Clarke): served by the system, no physical contact. Distinguish their actual relations vs images of them. Sometimes *both* sorts ignored; users not always “configured” (Woolgar) → methodological *and* normative issue.
- Following actors only: identifying relevant groups, **scale** (Joerges: analytical *and* practical problem), and success can become messy/impossible.
- Analysts must use their own categories *and* let actors’ concepts/theories in. Both have abstract and material.
- **Giddens, double hermeneutic:** social scientists must understand actors’ world *and* how actors interpret social-science theories. Give space to both. “Follow the actors” is rescued by the **higher principle of symmetry**. Do *not* make the analyst omniscient.
- Therefore: actors’ theoretical ideas — **including TD** — must be treated symmetrically with ours, even if antithetical.

## Back to technological determinism

Humanities/social sciences ignore a **thundering herd of elephants** when they dismiss TD’s role in actors’ views and actions. (Historians exempted: Smith & Marx; Oldenziel on shifting meaning of “technology” and US technocracy. Note 12: modernity literature often ignores technology itself — Brey: they treat it as means for capitalism/state/family, not as an institution; also lack tools/confidence.)

**Michael L. Smith:** scholars lament TD’s tenacity but rarely ask what needs it addresses. Compared to academy vs creationism: how can something so wrong-headed still sway people?

- Smith is **right** that we must understand needs/interests TD serves.
- Smith is **wrong** to call TD (or creationism) wrong-headed superstition / false consciousness.
- Bloor again: **true and false beliefs both need explanation**. Stay impartial. Explain why so many people still regard TD as true.
- Earlier: don’t only follow actors (or you miss users). Now: **do** follow actors in their commitment to TD.

**Most misleading/dangerous aspect:** equation of **technological change with progress** (Leo Marx on tech and progress). Histories show messy, ambiguous development. Actors often present projects as simple/straightforward — they *must*, to make things happen and justify action. Stories of systems that don’t work / aren’t used / aren’t successful are armory against TD (unless you hold an evolutionary view of progress). TD will **not** disappear; it is **useful for system builders**.

Endurance: in some analysts’ accounts, system builders’ actions, policy makers’ justifications. Danger of ignoring it: miss its **subtlety and variety**. Sometimes a table to thump realist credentials; sometimes a rapier against pompous pedants. Either way: take it more seriously.

**Main map: Smith & Marx, eds., *Does Technology Drive History?* (1994)**
- US history-of-technology professors; American equation of tech with progress and a *partial* loss of faith in it.
- **Hard–soft spectrum.**
  - **Hard:** agency imputed to technology itself / its intrinsic attributes → inescapable necessity. Optimists: many free choices, dream of progress. Pessimists: necessity’s iron hand, totalitarian nightmare.
  - **Soft:** tech in a “far more various and complex social, economic, political, and cultural matrix.” Wyatt: **vague, not really determinism** — just history that takes tech seriously.

**Heilbroner, “Do Machines Make History?” (1967 / 1994a–b)**
- Most avowedly TD of the contributors: ontological *and* methodological.
- Heuristic (not a normative prescription): start an unfamiliar society by examining **available machines**.
- TD “does not imply that human behaviour must be deprived of its core of consciousness and responsibility.”

**Edgerton:** TD = “the thesis that society is determined by **technology in use**” — includes societies with technology but *not* high rates of change.

**Bimber — three interpretations** (cf. Radder’s methodological/epistemological/ontological relativism)
1. **Normative:** Winner, Ellul, Habermas — tech autonomous/determining when its development norms are removed from political/ethical debate. Decoupling from political accountability is the worry.
2. **Nomological (very hard; Bimber’s only “true” TD):** given past/current tech + laws of nature, **only one possible future course of social change**. Technology = **artifacts only** (no knowledge of production or use — that would let society in).
3. **Unintended consequences:** actors can’t anticipate all effects. Bimber **dismisses** this as TD — true of many activities, not intrinsic to tech.
- Bimber uses this to **rescue Marx** from the TD charge (only nomological counts; Marx fails it). Wyatt agrees Marx is not a TD; MacKenzie 1984 already settled that.

**Hughes — “technological momentum”**
- Between technical determinism and social constructivism.
- “A technological system can be both a cause and an effect; it can shape or be shaped by society. As they grow larger and more complex, systems tend to be more shaping of society and less shaped by it.”
- Method: constructivism for emergence/development; **momentum** for later growth and the *appearance* of autonomy.

## Wyatt’s four types (table 7.2) — the list to memorize

### 1. Justificatory (mostly actors; all around us)
- Employers justifying downsizing/reorganization.
- How we narrate life change over 200 years.
- Frustration with automated call-response systems.
- **EU Information Society Forum (2000):** ICT/Internet have “practically cancelled the concept of time and distance”; digital economy “radically changing the way we live, work and communicate”; “no doubt about the benefits that will lead us to a better quality of life.”
- Edwards: **“ideology of technological determinism”** — managers’ belief that productivity gains and social transformation will be **automatic** results of computerization.

### 2. Descriptive (analysts who spot it in others)
- MacKenzie & Wajcman; Misa; Smith & Marx: reject TD as *their* explanation (inadequate power); rarely ask *why* others use it; write richer situated accounts instead.
- Variants they name: tech developed independently of social forces (Misa); tech causes social change (Misa; Smith & Marx); both parts (MacKenzie & Wajcman).
- **Freeman (1987):** more assertive — in some cases TD is **quite a good description of the historical record**; limited autonomy of S&T in determining economic developments.

### 3. Methodological (Heilbroner, Edgerton, Hughes)
- Heilbroner: start with technologies available to societies/organizations.
- Hughes: momentum as an analytic tool for technology-in-history.
- Edgerton: society determined by technology **in use**.
- STS (ANT, social constructivism, history of technology, innovation theory) all take technology seriously — deviant in parent disciplines for that reason.
- **Provocation / guilty secret:** “really we are all technological determinists. If we were not, we would have no object of analysis; our raison d’être would disappear.”
- Winner, preface to *Autonomous Technology*: institutions/machines one must oppose and modify even while having affection for them.

### 4. Normative (Bimber; Misa’s second version; implicit in Hughes’s momentum)
- Winner’s **autonomous technology**: so big and complex it is no longer amenable to social control. Decoupling from political accountability; triumph of technological rationality.
- Intra-STS fight: Winner (1993) accuses constructivists of abandoning the need to make tech and tech change **accountable**. She concludes with that accusation in mind.

## Conclusion

Staudenmaier (end of *Does Technology Drive History?*): treat artifacts as “crystallized moments of past human vision … buffeted by the swirl of passion, contention, celebration, grief and violence that makes up the human condition.”

STS studies people and things, and images of people and things. **Also study explanations of people and things.** Treat TD as seriously as we treat technology.

Not enough to dismiss TD as conceptually crude, as actors’ false consciousness, or as a bleak Nietzschean future. TD is still here and unlikely to disappear. It remains:
- in **justifications** of actors pushing a direction of change
- as a **heuristic** for organizing accounts of technological change
- in **public discourse** that makes technology opaque and **beyond political intervention and control**

She maps types **not** because TD is an adequate framework for social/technical relations, but because **lots of other actors think it is** — so we must understand manifestations and functions.

STS is not innocent of **methodological and normative** TD. The new obligation: stop being obtuse about **justificatory** TD. Only then: dynamics of sociotechnical systems + rhetorical devices of decision makers.

**Remaining STS challenges (unresolvable dialectics — keep wrestling):**
1. How machines make history **in concert with** current generations of people.
2. Dialectical relationship: **social shaping of technology** *and* **technical shaping of society**.
3. Treat analysts’ and actors’ categories symmetrically — even when actors’ category is TD.

Take seriously the messy heterogeneous collections (individuals, groups, artifacts, rules, knowledges) that make the sociotechnical world; why stabilization/extension sometimes succeeds and sometimes fails. **Only then will people have the tools to participate in creating a more democratic sociotechnical order.**

## Lines worth remembering

- “Technological determinism is dead; long live technological determinism.”
- Two parts, both necessary: tech develops **outside society** *and* tech change **determines social change**.
- Technological progress = social progress (the dangerous equation).
- TD endures because it is simple and it matches everyday experience.
- TD “absolves us from responsibility for the technologies we make and use.”
- “We have to recognize the technological determinists within ourselves.”
- Pinch & Bijker: working is the **result**, not the cause, of a machine becoming a successful artifact.
- Wyatt reverse: success is the **result**, not the cause, of a machine becoming a working artifact.
- “Follow the actors” rescued by a **higher principle of symmetry** (actors’ *and* analysts’ concepts — including TD).
- Smith & Marx: **hard** (agency in tech → inescapable necessity) vs **soft** (tech in a complex matrix — “not really determinism”).
- Heilbroner: start with the machines available; TD does not strip consciousness and responsibility.
- Edgerton: society determined by **technology in use**.
- Bimber: **normative / nomological / unintended consequences** — only nomological is “true” TD; artifacts only.
- Hughes: **technological momentum** — systems become more shaping of society as they grow.
- Four types: **justificatory, descriptive, methodological, normative**.
- “Our guilty secret in STS is that really we are all technological determinists.”
- Study explanations of people and things. TD remains in justifications, as a heuristic, and in discourse that puts tech **beyond political control**.
- Dialectics: social shaping of technology **and** technical shaping of society; wrestle, don’t resolve.
- End goal: “a more democratic sociotechnical order.”
