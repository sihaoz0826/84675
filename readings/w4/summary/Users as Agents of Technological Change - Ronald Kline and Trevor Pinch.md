# Users as Agents of Technological Change — Fast Read (all main points)

**Authors:** Ronald Kline and Trevor Pinch | **Technology and Culture** 37(4), Oct. 1996, pp. 763–795
**What this is:** A SCOT case study. Rural U.S. farm users did not just receive a finished car; they reopened a *stabilized* artifact (esp. the Model T), gave it new meanings, and forced manufacturers to respond with kits, trucks, and tractors.

**Core claim / thesis.** Users of technology acted as **agents of technological change**. Farm people were active participants in the social construction of the automobile. Use produced unforeseen consequences *and* helped reshape both the interpretation and the design of an artifact already treated as relatively stable.

---

## Setup (what they turn around)

- Historians treat America as a “car culture,” but write the car as an urban story: urban inventors, manufacturers, city pleasures, traffic, suburban sprawl.
- Rural auto histories have focused on social “impact” and the Ford/Model T fascination. The car is an **“external” force** that urbanized the countryside.
- Farm modifications get mentioned in passing, then take a backseat to **technological determinism**: autonomous tech drives social change.
- They reverse this. Shift the field from **producers** (inventors, engineers, manufacturers) to **users** (laborers, factory owners, homeworkers, consumers).
- Parallel user-shaping cases they cite: Douglas (radio amateurs: point-to-point → broadcasting); Fischer and Martin (telephone callers force a social, not just business, instrument); Nye (many social meanings of electricity). Their auto case goes further: users change **interpretation and design** of a *stable* artifact.

## SCOT (definitions to remember)

**SCOT** = Social Construction of Technology (Pinch and Bijker, 1980s). Advantages for studying users as agents.

- **Relevant social groups:** groups who share a **meaning** of the artifact. That meaning explains developmental paths. Groups are not static; new ones emerge. They may also share a Wittgensteinian **family resemblance** (e.g. women who shared the high-wheeler as an “unsafe machine”).
- **Technological frame** (Bijker): how individuals deviate from shared group meaning; people can be weakly or strongly included in several frames.
- **Interpretative flexibility:** the same artifact means different things to different groups. Bicycle: young male sport riders = **“macho machine”**; women and elderly men wanting transport = **“unsafe machine”** (headers / “doing a header”). Meanings get embedded in new designs (even larger wheels for speed).
- **Closure / stabilization:** some artifacts appear to have fewer problems and become dominant. Rivals need not vanish (jets and props coexist). Closure need not be final; new problems can reopen flexibility.
- SCOT is stronger than “tech is embedded in human affairs.” It asks what counts as a **working** artifact and a **satisfactory test**. Groups contest workability.
- Winner’s critique: SCOT’s group definition is too tight and ignores “irrelevant” groups. They reply: family resemblance lets historians recover marginalized groups (women, African Americans, etc.).

**Two SCOT weaknesses they fix here**
1. Original SCOT was mostly **design-stage**. Closure was too rigid. Missing: how/when the **black box** reopens at the **use** stage. Black box = design has *stabilized*, not that the guts are opaque. The Model T was a stabilized black box *designed to be easily repaired*.
2. Little on **power / social structure**, and little on the **reciprocal** relation: groups shape artifacts; using artifacts reconstitutes group identities.

**Their two moves**
1. Rural U.S. auto adaptation: interpretative flexibility **reappears after** stabilization.
2. Map group power, especially **gender**.

**Gender toolkit (Harding, via Cockburn and Wajcman):** gender **structure**, **identity**, **symbolism**. Wajcman: SCOT missed how the absence/weakness of female groups constructs tech as a **“masculine culture.”** Pinch and Bijker’s bicycle study already used gender (male speedsters vs. women riders). Here they show gender helping shape a *stabilized* artifact, then being reinforced by it.

## Interpretative flexibility revisited — the anticar crusade

Early cars in the countryside (rich city folk out for a spin) met hostility.

**Epithets / counters**
- Rural/small-town/suburban/city critics: **“red devil,” “devil wagon.”**
- Auto journals: **“hayseed,” “rube,” “autophobe,” “motorphobe.”**
- St. Louis 1905: Red Devil Automobile Club (defied anti-“scorcher” feeling).

**Grievances**
- Livestock: horses rear, buggies upset; chickens killed; ram charges a jacked Ford, breaks its neck (owner asks no compensation — the ram hit the car). Drivers often paid for killed animals.
- Farm women: reckless autos made horse-and-buggy roads unsafe. NY 1904: people who can own autos should **build their own roads**. Maine 1909: roads impassable half the year; other half “because of automobiles.” USDA 1915 survey of 2,000+ crop-correspondent wives: autos made country driving “hazardous or dangerous” (VT, NY, IL, WI, SC, WV, MS).
- Goggles/dusters looked monstrous (Utah “century run,” 1904: berry-picking family flees).
- Expensive, unreliable, loud (Indiana woman hears boyfriend start the car a quarter-mile away).
- Farmers tow broken cars / pull them from mud: income *and* moral satisfaction.
- Drivers = urban, upper class.
- Later fear: car wrecks the **horse-and-buggy institutional fabric** — consolidated schools, other churches, town visits instead of local church. (Wik: car actually increased church attendance; Berger more pessimistic.)

**Legal and illegal resistance**
- Counties in WV and PA **ban autos**; Vermont: person with a **red flag** must walk ahead.
- ~1908: slow/stop for frightened horses; **speed traps** begin; legislatures withhold road money.
- Farm men attack motorists, 1902–07: shot chauffeur (MN), stoned motorist (IN), shot at car (SC), assaulted chauffeur (WI); NY: iron pail, lawn mower in the path, whipping, fight at a Rochester hill-climb.
- Proprietary view of roads they built/maintained — then they **sabotage their own roads**: CT 1905 tire-cutting slag; MN plows up roads; IN 1909 weakens bridges/barricades (against late-night roadhouse traffic); Sacramento 1909 ditches catch **13** autos; rakes, saws, glass, tacks, ropes, barbed wire.
- Organizations: Farmers’ Anti-Automobile League near Evanston, IL (20-man vigilante committee); Anti-Automobile Club of Grover, MO; Farmers’ Protective Association, Harrison Township, OH.

**SCOT reading.** Wik’s **“anti-auto crusade”** = a relevant social group. Meaning is **“devil wagon,”** not later “flivver” (vibration good for the liver) or “Tin Lizzie.” This *is* radical interpretative flexibility: destroy cars / make roads impassable. If they had won, the car might have stayed a **short-distance city vehicle**; railroads would have kept rural transport; modern America would look different.

**Why the crusade failed**
- Urban luxury market saturates → cheaper cars for country roads.
- **Model T** (late 1908): high off the ground (easier repair), high hp/weight, three-point suspension.
- Farm-leader turn: National Grange summer 1908 — the “motor vehicle is a **permanent feature of modern life**” and has a right to rural roads. *Wallace’s Farmer* promotes gas autos from Jan. 1908 (ads, editorials, articles, reader experiences). Editor Feb. 1908: farmers had their “fun” with users; had called it the **“rich man’s plaything”**; now they buy. *Rural New Yorker* switches from critic to promoter, 1909.
- Two favored types: cheap high-wheel **buggy car**; touring car with **removable tonneau** → small truck (fig. 1, Moline ad).
- Better roads; end of isolation; tourist/repair income; farm men already ran steam and stationary gas engines.
- By **1920 Census:** farm households **30%** auto ownership vs. nonfarm **24%**.
- “Devil wagon” meaning **did not stabilize**.

Wik: not all rural people fought the car, but a large segment did. Flink: opposition localized, 1904–06, against *motoring* not the car — their evidence (multi-state, ~a decade, organized + unorganized) says it was widespread. No national leadership.

## Interpretative flexibility in the farm yard

Relevant groups here: **manufacturers, farm men, farm women** (+ emergent **car dealers**; they set aside home-demonstration agents and agricultural engineers).

By 1909 the fundamental design had stabilized: **“large, front-engined, rear-drive automobile” of system Panhard**. Further mid-1920s stabilization: electric starters, closed bodies, all-steel bodies. Manufacturers initially had the most influence over meaning — but not total: new makers could build different cars; **users can embed new meanings** once the artifact is in their hands.

**Stationary power (from 1903)**
- George Schmidt (KS, *Rural New Yorker* 1903): block up the hind axle, belt one wheel to a corn sheller, grinder, saw, pump, “or any other machine… and be in style with any city man.”
- T. A. Pottinger (IL, 1909): ideal farm car = detachable backseat (truck) + “light power” for sheller, ensilage cutter, grinding.
- Domestic: washing machines (suburban 1910 comic disaster: car falls off jack, drags washer and servant). Fig. 2: Kansas farmer Bill Ott, ~1930, Model T jacked to run a washer; daughter Lizzie Ott still operates the machine.
- Also “women’s work”: butter churn, cream separator — but **men** usually supplied the power, mainly for **men’s** field/shop jobs.
- Machines they powered: corn shellers, water pumps, hay balers, fodder and ensilage cutters, wood saws, hay and grain hoists, cider presses, corn grinders. One rancher sheared sheep with a **Cadillac**.
- Maine 1915: so many uses that tax assessors could not decide **pleasure vehicle vs. agricultural machinery**.
- Rare urban parallels: Locke Machine works (Detroit) powered the plant with two jacked Fords in a coal strike; cars used as building hoists.

**Mobile unexpected uses:** snowmobiles, tractors, agricultural transport, hay rake, pickup, feed chopper. Oral history: 7 of 23 NY farm families recalled car-as-rake/truck/power. Winfred Arnold (88): neighbors used the car for power; he could afford stationary gas engines.

**Punchline.** Flexibility returns **not at the design stage**. New users — **technically competent farm men** — give new meanings. Urban user: transport. Rural user: transport **and/or** farm tool, stationary power, domestic technology.

## Gendering the car

Gender relations on farms were fairly stable despite cultural diversity.

**Gender structure**
- 19th c.: men head farm and family; control productive and reproductive labor; commercial farming.
- Turn of century: women gain some public/domestic control with “modernization,” but sexual division of labor persists.
- Men (husband, sons, hired hands): main income work — field, barn, machine shop.
- Women (wife, daughters, hired help): “supportive” work — house, garden, poultry. Dairy often shared.
- Field work at harvest / labor shortage, and egg/vegetable/dairy cash, framed as **“helping out”** so the farmstead stands.
- Before WWII, women often accepted mechanizing **men’s** field jobs before **their** housework — not without protest.

**Gender identity**
- Many farm men, esp. Midwest, saw themselves as mechanics: operate, maintain, repair, redesign (steam engines, threshers, kitchen pumps).
- Mechanical competence = defining **masculinity** for farm men, craftsmen, linotype operators, small entrepreneurs.
- Women might pump water, drive the buggy, occasionally run field machines; **men** fixed the pump, greased the buggy, redesigned a hay binder for hills.

**Gender symbolism + access**
- Gasoline auto already **symbolically inscribed for masculine use** by Ford et al. Model T = “man’s car” (hard to steer/shift; no driver’s-side front door). Women buyers in MD and NH avoided it.
- Early car = latest sophisticated farm machine → **province of men**.
- Driving extremes: Alice Guyer — father gave up, older sister drove; Bertha Pampel — “My dad never did drive. My mother did all the driving.” Opposite: Laura Drake — “Nobody touched that [car] but him.” ≥2 of 23 NY families: mother or daughter never learned. Some accounts: daughters drove as much as brothers.
- Motor-wise farm woman = news (NY 1915: woman vulcanizes a flat). Men did most maintenance/tinkering (few rural repair shops). Average farm man ≠ expert, but thought better with cars than city men.

**Who reopens the black box**
- Farm man’s competence, rooted in masculine identity → jack up rear wheels → power “men’s” work, less often “woman’s” separator/pump/washer.
- Fig. 2 as the gendered division in one image: **he** jacks the car; **she** still runs the washer.
- **Only one exception** found: independent woman farmer, 1918, used the car to pull a hay rake.
- Men also convert passenger car → produce truck, then restore the manufacturer’s “feminine” passenger form and hand it to women (sometimes to fetch tractor parts).

**Mutual shaping.** Men’s identity (vs. constructed femininity) enables flexible interpretation (car = stationary power). That construction **re-masculinizes** technical competence. Gender shapes the car; the car shapes gender identities.

**Farm women: independence vs. “more work for mother”**
- Some historians: car extends women’s sphere — wider marketing, more household economic power, visits beyond the farmstead.
- “Modernizers” (home economists, farm-journal editors, auto makers) push Country Life ideology of saving the overworked farm woman.
- Mrs. Arthur Hewins (MA, 1920): in “our ‘Lizzie’” she hauls milk 3 miles daily, pleasure rides, movies 9 miles away.
- Other historians (Jellison, Neth): travel farther but stay in **supportive** roles (shop, emergency tractor parts). Neth ≈ Fischer on the telephone: car **enhances** traditional rural social ties, does not replace them. Parallel to suburban women (Cowan, Wajcman).
- Cowan question: **“more work for mother”?** Pre-1945 time-use studies *are* of farm women (the “overworked farm woman” problem) but say little about auto use; sample is atypically well-to-do / urban-domestic. Still: ~2,000 women, most families with autos, still work a **full week** (house, dairy, garden, poultry).
- L. B. Pierce (*Rural New Yorker*, 1919): woman cooks in a **fireless cooker**, drives 41 miles to Cleveland, shops, home for late supper; also runs a butter-and-egg route. Before the car she had to skimp morning work and husband got his own dinner. After: wash dishes, sweep, hit the route *and* serve dinner. Car lets her **do more work** inside an **expanded** “woman’s work” sphere (business + city bargains + kinship).

## Stability of social meanings

| Meaning | Trajectory |
|---|---|
| Anticar / devil wagon | Intense, **transient**; dies when cheap, road-capable cars arrive |
| Car destroys stores, one-room schools, local churches | Dies because the countryside **is** transformed that way. 1920–40: car enlarges the radius of rural life |
| **Gender meanings** | **Stable.** Flexibility of the early auto reinforced them |

Truck and tractor do not upset gender structure. WWI: urban women in the **American Woman’s Land Army** (“farmerette” squads) drive/maintain tractors; farm women drive at home for the “manpower” shortage; women build tractors in factories. After the war, farming by horse, car, or tractor is still **men’s work**.

Rural electrification + post-WWII farm prosperity: electric washers, ranges, irons, refrigerators. Jellison: less house/garden/poultry/dairy work + tractors replacing hired men → option to drive tractors or take a town job. Still framed as **“helping out.”** Country people weave new artifacts into a **flexible, durable** rural gender system.

## The restabilization of the car

Four commercial groups answer users who opened the black box: **auto makers, farm-equipment makers, gasoline-engine firms, accessory companies**. Timing shows they are responding to farm interpretative flexibility.

**Most auto makers counteract flexibility**
- Removable tonneaus: yes (pre-WWI). Jacked-up stationary power: usually **no** (engine/differential damage).
- *Rural New Yorker* 1906 survey: **6 of 7** makers adamantly opposed. REO more equivocal: disapproves traction-engine/sawmill use, but farmers could “save a considerable amount of labor” if they harnessed power properly. Firms: Electric Vehicle, Knox, Pope, Nordyke & Marmon, Winton, Franklin.
- Journal then tells farmers to buy a **stationary gasoline engine** (which it advertises), despite farm letters of “good luck.” 1919: shifts to recommending **safe kits** (crankshaft or rear axle) now advertised. Ag engineers F. H. King (1907) and Robert Smith (1920s) warn homemade kits wreck engines/differentials.

**Ford is more positive**
- 1903 photo: Henry Ford saws wood with a new Model A; pulley on a shaft into a side crankshaft connection.
- 1908: *Ford Times* photo of a Model S sawing wood.
- H. B. Harper: Model T specs match farm needs; “with a little ingenuity the engine [of any automobile] can be made to run the cream separator, saw the wood or pull a trailer.” Slogan: **“universal car.”** Stories of Tin Lizzies plowing, grinding, filling silos, etc.
- 1912 poem (Peoria) “The Auto on the Farm”: milks, washes, reaps, threshes, plows, pumps, grinds, hauls the baby, runs the dynamo. *Ford Times* retitles it **“Farming a la Ford.”**
- WWI postcard series **“Let Lizzie Do It”** (washer, plow; women at the wheel). Ford does not discourage free publicity (same stance as Ford-joke books). Maud Muller card: farm woman now “up-to-date” because she plows with a Ford.

**Accessory kits commercialize user practice**
- Power-takeoff kits from 1912; ads explode **1917** (wartime labor/horse shortages).
- Problem: jacking **one** wheel strains the differential. Fixes: jack both sides and take axle power (*Scientific American* 1917); Lawrence Auto Power (St. Paul) **$35** crankshaft kit (tie-rod, two pulleys, stand) — grinder, sheller, silo filler, saw, separator — no jacking.
- Other crankshaft kits 1917–19: E.F. Elmberg (Parkersburg, IA); Ward Work-a-Ford (Lincoln, NE); Auto Power and Malleable (Omaha); Knight Metal Products (Detroit) — kit + Ford **cheaper than a 14 hp stationary engine**. Maxim Silencer wood-saw kit still sold **1948**.

**Tractor conversion kits (1917 flurry)**
- Govt: **“plow to the fences.”**
- Prewar DIY: 1903 Ohioan plows with chauffeur-driven touring car (*Scientific American*); 1916 Montana barber-farmer with a Model T; 1915 A. W. Bell (Canada) turns a 3-year-old Overland into a “tractor” with larger steel reaper wheels and a heavier axle.
- Kits copy Bell: tractor drive wheels, heavy axle, reduction gears (~**3 mph**), large radiator, forced-feed lubrication, anti-overheat. **$97.50–$350**. Claim: Model T → field tractor (plows, harrows, mowers, binders) or road traction engine (graders, wagons). “Any Auto” (fig. 4) adds a pulley so you need not jack a wheel.
- **22 companies**, incl. Smith-Form-A-Tractor and Uni-Ford (IL); American Ford-A-Tractor and Hardy Hank (MN); Geneva Tractor (OH); F. R. Corcoran (NY); L. A. Tractor (CA).
- Most short-lived. **Pullford** (Quincy, IL): $135 kit, 1917, advertised through at least **1940**. Pullford + Shaw Mfg. (Galesburg, KS) in the Depression target men rich enough for an *old* car, too poor for a tractor. 1940 fits: Model T, Model A, 1926–31 Chevy, 1932 Ford V-8. Ad image shifts 1930: cheerful youth with reconvertible “flivver” → middle-aged “professional farmer” with a **permanent workhorse**.
- SCOT: kit makers **commercialize what farm men already did**. Ford still gets 1930s–40s letters about homemade Model T/A tractors.
- Nicknames (oral history): **“Skeeter”** (South), **“Puddle Jumper”** (Midwest), **“Doodle-Bug”** (Northeast).

**Ford vs. dealers (contractual closure)**
- Ambivalent before Ford’s own truck/tractor line. 1908: secretary says “ordinary automobiles” already used for stationary power; Ford is designing a tractor. 1912: *Ford Times* shows Model T + Home Auto Kit sawing wood. 1919: car-tractor-truck combo “we do not believe would work satisfactorily.”
- Once Ford sells the farm **ensemble — car, truck, tractor** — it wants no multi-use kits.
- 1916 sales letter: do not convert “Ford cars into trucks and other makeshifts not recommended or sanctioned by us” — **lose the dealership**. 1917: don’t sell truck kits; Ford truck is out. 1918: alterations **void the warranty**. Kit sales still climb.
- **Ford dealers** = new relevant social group: car as **multipurpose vehicle** (shared with kit makers and many farmers), but **subservient by contract**. One group uses **contractual power** as a **closure mechanism** (Bijker/Giddens: power = capacity to harness others’ agency). Limit: dealers can leave Ford.

**Dedicated machines as the real closure**
- Most effective response: **redesign tractors and trucks in WWI**. Pre-1912 gas tractors inherited heavy, costly steam-tractor flaws; industry nearly collapsed 1912. Then smaller, cheaper tractors (Ford among them).
- By 1940: mass tractors with **belt-power** (popular), **rubber tires**, **three-point hitch** (stops light tractors rearing) displace horses, steam, auto power kits, and auto-tractor kits.

**Tractor industry vs. kits**
- Dismiss kits as impractical, but they were a threat. Pullford advertised a win vs. gas tractors at the **1917 Fremont, NE** national plow contest. Kits at shows through Nov. 1919; four entered **1918 Salina, KS** contest.
- Late 1918: **American Tractor Association** asks the War Industries Board to “**prohibit entirely** the manufacture of attachments for converting automobile and motor trucks into tractors for farm use.” Williams says the ban succeeded; Kline and Pinch: unclear whether WIB accepted that clause.
- Henry Ford ran tractor experiments on auto engines; wanted the **Fordson**, not kits.
- La Crosse Tractor ad manager (1918): use a stationary gas engine for washers; save tractor belt power for big jobs. Jacking a car to belt-drive a grindstone or separator is “**simply ridiculous**.”

**Trucks and gas engines**
- Same closure pattern: criticize alternative uses + sell dedicated products.
- WWI makes the truck a serious, sturdy, mass-capacity product. Ford **1-ton truck, 1917** + warranty policy kill Model T truck conversions.
- Stationary-engine firms: powerful but costly/hard to start; now compete with auto/tractor PTO. **Maytag 1915:** kick-start gas engine on washers. Johnson: only then did farm “women begin to feel at home with [gasoline] engines.” Oral histories: women still needed a man to start balky washers. Gas or electric Maytags sold into the 1940s (rural electrification). People who could afford one stopped jacking the car to wash.

**Why the auto still dominated farm power to WWII**
- Census: autos were far and away the most popular inanimate farm power, 1920–war.
- 1920s–30s farm crisis: families reuse WWI-boom cars for town, hauling, belt work, even field work (bought or homemade kits).
- Prosperous farms *did* buy tractors/trucks/engines. **538 well-to-do Minnesota farms, 1929:** >90% autos; ~2/3 stationary gas engines; nearly 1/2 tractors; >1/3 electricity; ~1/3 trucks. Autos used almost equally for farm and family; study **does not mention** belt-power use of the car — they used tractors, engines, motors, trucks for that.
- 1930s **New Deal** low-cost farm-equipment loans → tractor boom → displaces the rural auto as all-purpose power.

## Conclusion (their own)

SCOT tools used: relevant social groups, interpretative flexibility, closure. Extension: a **well-stabilized** artifact reopened by users; gender explains how flexibility appears at the use stage.

Mutually constructed **gender relations** and **manufacturer–dealer–buyer** transactions both **constrained and enabled** design and use.

**Paradoxes**
- A **masculinized** gender position lets farm men open the black box and threaten the car’s predominant meaning — and those new options **reinforce** predominant gender identities.
- For manufacturers, new flexibility is first a **threat**, then **new markets**: dedicated machines for each usage.
- Differentiation is not the only response. Electric car for women **fails**; makers adapt the **gasoline** car (electric starter, closed top) and enlarge the existing market. Meaning changes for women users; Scharff: the new artifact **unlikely** significantly altered gender *relations*. But it shifted **identities**: women get new freedom; men need not be quite so manly (no cranking risk).

Story of users and technology should track **radical options for change** and how other groups answer them by creating **new artifacts**.

Interpretative flexibility of the rural car **gone by the early 1950s**. Closure again: farm people stop grinding, plowing, or hauling produce with the auto. They buy **tractors and pickup trucks** — artifacts manufacturers developed partly in response to those novel interpretations.

Last line: **“The users, so easily overlooked in writing the story of technology, had made their mark.”**

## Lines worth remembering

- Users are **agents of technological change**, not just victims of “impact.”
- SCOT trio: **relevant social groups / interpretative flexibility / closure**. Closure is not forever; the black box can reopen **at the use stage**.
- Black box = **stabilized design**, not necessarily opaque guts. Model T: stable *and* user-repairable.
- Groups shape artifacts; using artifacts **reconstitutes group identities** (esp. gender).
- Harding’s three: **structure, identity, symbolism**.
- Anticar meaning: **“devil wagon”** vs. later **“flivver” / “Tin Lizzie.”** If the crusade had won: city-only car, railroads to the country.
- Grange 1908: the motor vehicle is a **“permanent feature of modern life.”**
- 1920: farm auto ownership **30% > nonfarm 24%**.
- Urban meaning = transport. Rural meaning = transport **+ farm tool + stationary power + domestic tech**.
- Schmidt 1903: jack it, belt it, “be in style with any city man.”
- Men reopen the box; women (with one 1918 exception) do not. Fig. 2: he jacks, she washes.
- Gender shapes the car; the car **re-masculinizes** competence.
- Cowan on the farm: the car enables **more work for mother** in an expanded sphere (Pierce / fireless cooker).
- Ford slogan: **“universal car.”** Poem retitled **“Farming a la Ford.”** Postcards: **“Let Lizzie Do It.”**
- Homemade tractor nicknames: **Skeeter / Puddle Jumper / Doodle-Bug**.
- Ford dealers share the multipurpose meaning but Ford uses **contractual power** (franchise + warranty) as closure.
- ATA 1918: try to **prohibit** conversion attachments.
- La Crosse: jacking a car to run a cream separator is “**simply ridiculous**.”
- Dedicated trucks/tractors/engines **eliminate** interpretative flexibility — and those products exist partly **because users invented the uses**.
- Flexibility gone by **early 1950s**. “The users… had made their mark.”
