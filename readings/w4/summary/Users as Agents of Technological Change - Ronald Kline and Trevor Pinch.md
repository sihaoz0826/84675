# Users as Agents of Technological Change — Fast Read

**Authors:** Ronald Kline and Trevor Pinch | **Technology and Culture** 37(4), Oct. 1996

**What this is:** A history paper about early cars on U.S. farms. The authors use SCOT (Social Construction of Technology) to show that users can remake a machine after it already looks “finished.”

**Thesis:** Farmers did not just receive a finished car. They changed what it meant and how it was built, so users are agents of technological change.

---

## Main points

- Most car histories are city stories: inventors, factories, traffic, suburbs. Rural histories often treat the car as an outside force that “hit” the countryside. The authors flip this. Look at users, not just makers.

- **SCOT in plain words:** different groups see the same machine differently. Those meanings can steer what gets built. Later, one meaning and one design usually win. That settling is called closure. It is not forever. New users can reopen a “settled” machine.

- Two gaps they fix. First, older SCOT mostly watched design, not everyday use. Second, it said little about power. Using a machine also remakes who people are — especially along gender lines.

- Early farm people often hated cars. They called them “devil wagons”: they scared horses, killed livestock, and made roads unsafe. Some counties banned cars. Some farmers sabotaged their own roads. If that side had won, the car might have stayed a short-range city vehicle, with railroads still serving the country.

- That fight failed. Cheap, high, tough cars (especially the Model T) could handle farm roads. Farm leaders accepted the car. By 1920, more farm homes owned a car than nonfarm homes.

- **The core farm story:** farm men jacked up the rear wheels and belted the spinning wheel to a mill, saw, pump, or washer. In the city, a car meant a ride. On the farm, it meant a ride, a truck, and a stationary engine.

- Farm men could do this because mechanical skill was part of being a man on the farm. They reopened the black box (a settled design, not a sealed mystery — the Model T was built to be fixed). Women usually still ran the washer. Men converted the car to a truck, then put the seats back and handed it to women. Gender shaped the car; the car also remade gender.

- For farm women, the car often meant more travel — and more work. They still cooked, cleaned, sold eggs, and kept family ties, just over a wider area.

- Gender meanings stuck. The “devil wagon” meaning faded. Later trucks and tractors did not flip who did farm work. Even after women drove tractors in World War I, field machines stayed “men’s work.”

- Companies answered the farm uses. Most car makers said: do not jack it, you will break it. Ford liked the “universal car” story. Kit makers sold pulleys and tractor conversions. Once Ford sold its own truck and tractor, it used dealer contracts and warranties to kill the kits.

- The lasting answer was dedicated machines: tractors, trucks, and engines. By the early 1950s, people had mostly stopped grinding, plowing, or hauling with the family car. Those new machines exist partly because users invented the uses first.

---

## Lines worth remembering

- Users are agents of change, not just people “impacted” by tech.
- A settled machine can be reopened at the use stage.
- City car = transport. Farm car = transport + engine + tool.
- Gender shaped the car; the car also remade gender (men as the tinkerers).
- Companies first fought the new uses, then sold dedicated machines for them.
- “The users, so easily overlooked… had made their mark.”
