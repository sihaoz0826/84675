# The Linear Model of Innovation — Benoît Godin (2006)

**What this is:** A history of how people came to think of innovation as a straight line: basic research → applied research → development → product (and then spread).

**Thesis:** Vannevar Bush did not invent this model. Industry people, consultants, and business schools built it; economists later stretched it. It is still around because official statistics count research as those three steps in a row, so the story feels like a fact.

- **The linear model, in everyday words:** First scientists chase knowledge for its own sake (basic research). Then they try to solve a real problem (applied research). Then engineers turn that into something you can make (development). Then a company sells it and it spreads (product / diffusion). Each step is treated as depending on the one before.
- People have used, improved, or attacked this story for decades, but almost no one cites a first author. Many point to Bush’s 1945 report *Science: The Endless Frontier*. Godin says Bush only claimed that basic science leads to social and economic progress. He never laid out the full step-by-step chain.
- The story was built in three waves. Early 1900s–1945: “pure” science is the seed; applied work grows from it. 1930s–1960: add **development**, the everyday work of industrial labs. 1950s on: economists add making the product and spreading it.
- After World War I, firms built labs. Public talk still praised “pure” science as the source of future inventions (J. J. Carty at AT&T is the classic example). Inside the firm, though, there was little basic research, some applied work, and a lot of development.
- By the 1940s, industry writers were already drawing flow charts: science → applied work → development → the factory. Government number-crunchers then simplified that into the three official boxes: basic, applied, development.
- Early research surveys were sloppy (firms decided what “research” meant). In the 1950s the U.S. National Science Foundation, then the OECD’s Frascati rules, locked in the three boxes and counted money in that order. Development always ate most of the budget.
- Those counts were meant for bookkeeping. They also became a process story: NSF said each stage depends on the one before. Once the categories were official, the model hardened into something people treated as real.
- Economists arrived late. They borrowed the three boxes, then added “innovation” (first commercial use) and “diffusion” (spread). Schumpeter himself said invention and innovation need not follow each other; later writers lined them up anyway.
- Critics have said the straight-line picture is false since the 1960s. Rival models with feedback loops look messy and have no matching official numbers, so they do not replace it. Simple stories that come with statistics last.

## Lines worth remembering

- Basic research → applied research → development → product (and spread).
- Bush said basic science is the “pacemaker” of progress. That is a two-part claim, not a full model.
- Industry added development; government statistics made the three-step story official.
- The model lives on because it is simple and because it is how we count R&D money.
- No numbers, no replacement: other models cannot easily knock it off the books.
