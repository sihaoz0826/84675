# The Linear Model of Innovation — Fast Read (all main points)

**Author:** Benoît Godin (INRS, Montreal) | **Science, Technology, & Human Values** 31(6), Nov. 2006, pp. 639–667
**Subtitle:** The Historical Construction of an Analytical Framework
**Keywords:** linear model of innovation; basic research; innovation; statistics; science policy
**What this is:** A history-of-ideas article. Godin reconstructs how the linear model of innovation was built, by whom, and why it still structures science policy and statistics even after everyone has declared it dead.

**Core claim / thesis.** The linear model owes little to Vannevar Bush. It is a theoretical construction of industrialists, consultants, and business schools, seconded by economists. It developed in three stages (and three communities). It survives because official statistics — NSF, then OECD/Frascati — counted research as three sequential components and thereby turned the model into a “social fact.” Rival models lack statistical foundations, so they cannot easily replace it.

**The model itself:**
> Basic research → Applied research → Development → (Production and) Diffusion

---

## Opening: everyone says it is dead — is it?

- One of the first conceptual/theoretical frameworks for science, technology, and the economy.
- Source is nebulous. Users, improvers, and critics for fifty years rarely cited an original. Usually taken for granted.
- Common attribution: Bush, *Science: The Endless Frontier* ([1945] 1995). Godin: Bush has only a *rudiment* — causal talk that basic research yields socioeconomic progress. No sequential process, no mechanism.
- Rosenberg (1994): “Everyone knows that the linear model of innovation is dead.” Godin asks: is this really the case?
- Academic lobbies (NSF 1957a) and economists (Nelson 1959) disseminated it and used it to justify public support for science. Science policies carried a linear conception for many decades (Mowery 1983a).

**Three historical stages (the spine of the article):**
1. **Early 20th c. → c. 1945:** basic + applied. Ideal of pure science; causal link from basic to applied. Rhetoric Bush inherited from industrialists and the U.S. National Research Council.
2. **1934 → c. 1960 (core of the paper):** add **development**. Standard three-stage model: Basic → Applied → Development. Analytical *and* statistical reasons.
3. **1950s onward:** extend to non-R&D — production and diffusion. Economists from business schools.

**Why it lives:** statistical categories for counting and allocating money; standardized under OECD methodological manuals. Functioned as a **social fact**. Rivals could not substitute easily.

**Four parts:** (1) political rhetoric / pure-science ideal; (2) “development” in industrial research; (3) crystallization via statistics; (4) economists add innovation.

**U.S. focus** (with foreign contributors when they matter): Americans first formalized it as a sequential model; U.S. first collected the statistics systematically.

**Against Edgerton (2004):** “The linear model is very hard to find anywhere, except in some descriptions of what it is supposed to have been.” Godin agrees it is not in Bush — but not that it is nowhere. Look past the *name*: the model, whatever called, has been *the* mechanism for explaining innovation in the technological-change literature since the late 1940s.

## A political rhetoric (pure over applied)

- Greeks onward: intellectual vs. practical as opposites. *Theoria* valued over practice. Network of dichotomies (Arendt; Lloyd; Lobkowicz).
- Scientists’ version: superiority of **pure** over **applied**. “Pure research” dated to 1648 (I. B. Cohen 1948): abstract natural philosophy vs. “mixed” subjects (mixed mathematics) (Kline 1995). Regular use by late 19th c., paired with applied research.
- Opposition is by **motive** (knowledge for its own sake). Rhetoric used to:
  - define/demarcate/control a profession (exclude amateurs)
  - get money (scientists)
  - raise a discipline’s status (engineers)
  - attract scientists (industrialists)
- Also a British freedom-of-science rhetoric, against Nazi Germany and to some extent the Soviet Union (Congress for Cultural Freedom 1955).
- At the same time, one-way *cooperation*: “Basic research was the seed from which applied research grew.”
  - Rowland (1902, 594): “To have the applications of a science, the science itself must exist.”

**Industrial research after WWI:**
- Big firms build labs. Governments help.
- Britain: Department of Scientific and Industrial Research funds industrial research orgs.
- U.S.: National Research Council. Links to industry from 1916 war prep. After the war, NRC “impressed by the great importance of promoting the application of science to industry” created an Industrial Research Division (Barrows 1941, 367). 1920s: “hotbed of activity, preaching to corporations the benefits of funding their own research” (Zachary). Studies, lab visits for executives, conferences, helped found the Industrial Research Institute (1938/39; independent 1945; still exists), biennial lab directory 1920–mid-1950s.

**J. J. Carty (AT&T VP) — type specimen of the industrialist rhetoric:**
- Pure research “of incalculable value to all the industries” (1916).
- 1924, U.S. Chamber of Commerce: “The future of American business and commerce and industry is dependent upon the progress of science.”
- Science = two kinds, pure and applied. Pure scientists = “the advance guard of civilization”; they furnish raw material to engineers/chemists for business, industry, commerce.
- Refuses to debate the terms: “the two researches are conducted in exactly the same manner.” Distinction is **motives**.
- First full-length rationale for public support of pure research (*Science*, 1916). Pure science = “the seed of future great inventions.” Benefits “indirect, intangible or remote” → “natural home” is the university. Master scientists should get every resource money can buy. Money from philanthropists (and later the State) and from industry. Near von Humboldt’s 1809 university memorandum.

**Bush, *Science: The Endless Frontier* ([1945] 1995):**
- Blueprint: create a National Research Foundation to support basic research publicly, regularly.
- Rhetoric is all socioeconomic benefits: jobs, wages, shorter hours, crops, leisure, living standards, disease, conservation, defense. “Without scientific progress no amount of achievement in other directions can insure our health, prosperity, and security as a nation in the modern world.”
- Basic research = “performed without thought of practical ends”; yields “general knowledge and an understanding of nature and its laws.” Must be related to applied: industrial development “would eventually stagnate if basic research were long neglected.”
- Famous line: basic research “creates the fund from which the practical applications of knowledge must be drawn. … basic research is the pacemaker of technological progress.”
- Furthest Bush goes. At best: Basic research → applied research (technology). Bowman appendix taxonomy (pure / background / applied and development) used only to estimate the funding gap, *not* as a sequential model of progress.
- Bush put the ideal on officials’ lips. He did **not** invent a model unless a one-way link between two variables counts as a model.

## An industrial perspective (add “development”)

- Public industrialist talk was recruitment: “science” or “research.” Inside firms: little basic, some applied, **a lot of development**.
- “Development” is an **industry term** (Godin 2005a). 1920s: “departments of applied science” / “departments of development and research.” Then “research and development.” 1930s company reports join the two words.
- Development usually treated as part of (applied) research or engineering. NRC 1920: research and development “commonly merge”; “research” often applied to mere development of processes, methods, equipment, production, by-products.
- Until WWII, almost no separate research vs. (product) development departments. Same people: engineers. After 1945 some large labs split the functions.
- Bernal: hard to distinguish scientists from technicians in industry. Engineers translate already-established scientific results into practical/economic terms.

**Early sequential “models” (usually diagrams):**
- **Mees (1920, Eastman Kodak):** development lab = small-scale manufacturing to take a new process/product to large-scale readiness. Sequence: pure research in the scientific department → practical research on laboratory scale → special development departments as intermediate stage → manufacturing.
- **Stevens (1941, Arthur D. Little / *Research: A National Resource*):** Godin calls this the first and most complete description. “Stages through which research travels on its way toward adoption of results in industry.” Stages 3–4 ≈ development:
  1. Fundamental research
  2. Applied research
  3. Test-tube or bench research
  4. Pilot plant
  5. Production
  6. Improvement
  7. Trouble shooting
  8. Technical control of process and quality
- **Bichowsky (1942):** “flow sheet chart” — research; engineering/development; factory/production.
- **Furnas (1948, Industrial Research Institute):** exploratory + fundamental → applied → development → production.

These efforts culminate in: **Basic research → Applied research → Development.** Official (government) statistics made this simpler, now-standardized model.

## A statistical classification (why the model crystallized)

**Thesis of the section:** 1920–1950 official statisticians built a three-component classification — basic / applied / development. That story is the key to crystallization. Statistics solidified a model-in-progress into a taken-for-granted **social fact**.

**Early measurement was sloppy:**
- “What is research?” left to the respondent.
- NRC lab directory 1920: “liberal interpretation”; include any lab that “by a liberal interpretation do any research work.” Holland & Spraragen (1933) and WPA (Perazich & Field 1940) inherit the mess.
- NRC 1941 and Canada’s Dominion Bureau of Statistics 1941: still no real definition.

**Cleanup: NSF + OECD/Frascati**
- 1951: NSF legally mandated to measure S&T. Defines research as “systematic, intensive study directed toward fuller knowledge … and the systematic use of that knowledge for the production of useful materials, systems, methods, or processes” (1953).
- OECD Frascati Manual 1963 (definition quoted from 1970 ed.; 1962 first edition had *no* definition of research): “creative work undertaken on a systematic basis to increase the stock of scientific and technical knowledge and to use this stock of knowledge to devise new applications.”

**Two pre-definition practices:**
1. Define by excluding routine work, or by a list (basic, applied, plus engineering, testing, prototypes, design — later lumped as development). No breakdowns. Early interest was *where* R&D was happening, not dollar magnitudes (Shapley 1959).
2. Define by **categories**. Basic taxonomy: pure vs. applied. Three typical measurement cases:
   - **No numbers:** Bernal, *The Social Function of Science* (1939) — cannot draw the line between pure and applied expenditures.
   - **Proxies:** Bush uses “basic research” = “without thought of practical ends.” Claims nation spends ~6× more on applied than basic — by equating university = basic, industry+government = applied. Appendix ratios (5% industry / 15% government / 70% colleges) have no methodology.
   - **Reject the taxonomy:** *Research: A National Resource* (1938) refuses to distinguish; fundamental and applied interact and both yield both kinds of results.

**Huxley (1934) — first formal taxonomy (Table 1):**
- background, basic, ad hoc, development
- Background: “no practical objective consciously in view”
- Basic: “quite fundamental, but has some distant practical objective”
- Those two = “what is usually called pure science”
- Ad hoc = applied
- Development ≈ translate lab findings into full-scale commercial practice
- Huxley did not measure. Influences: Bush borrows “basic”; OECD later “oriented basic research”; then measurement.

**Table 1 — taxonomies of research (remember the drift to three terms):**
| Source | Categories |
|---|---|
| Huxley 1934 | background, basic, ad hoc, development |
| Bernal 1939 | pure (and fundamental), applied |
| Bush 1945 | basic, applied |
| Bowman (in Bush) | pure, background, applied and development |
| U.S. PSRB 1947 | fundamental, background, applied, development |
| Canadian DRS 1947 | pure, background, applied, development, analysis and testing |
| Anthony | uncommitted, applied, development |
| U.S. NSF 1953 | basic, applied, development |
| British DSIR 1958 | basic, applied and development, prototype |
| OECD 1962 | fundamental, applied, development |

**First measurements:**
- **U.S. President’s Scientific Research Board (1947):** first real survey of resources for “R&D” — first time that term appears in a statistical report. Huxley-inspired: fundamental, background, applied, development. Could not get “precisely accurate” expenditures (different defs/accounting). Basic ≈ **4%** of U.S. research spending. University far below government/industry. Applied ≈ **90%**. Development *not* measured separately — folded into applied.
- **Canada DRS (1947):** **first measurement of development per se.** Research = pure + background + applied (not separated “because of the close inter-relationships”). Background here, as in Bush, means data collection/analysis. Development = work after lab-level research to the point of practical application or commercial production. Motive: military procurement. Of $34 million federal scientific activities: **40% research / 48% development / 12% analysis and testing**. Canada did not repeat this for years and never measured industrial development before Frascati (1962). 1938 U.S. government-research report had not used “development.”

**Anthony / Harvard Business School (the crystallization):**
- Problem: too much variation in what counts as research; firms differ on expenses. Routine usually out; messy frontier: testing, pilot plants, design, market studies in or out.
- Early 1950s: DoD Research and Development Board wants to locate industrial R&D contractors in event of war. Anthony + BLS: three surveys, published 1953.
- Anthony’s three items (output-centered):
  - **Uncommitted research:** planned search for new knowledge, with or without a specific application
  - **Applied research:** apply existing knowledge to create a new product/process (incl. evaluating uses)
  - **Development:** apply existing knowledge to *improve* a present product/process
- Include in development: scale activity, pilot plants, design. Exclude: market research, legal work, technical services, production.
- Industry spend: **8%** uncommitted/basic, **42%** new products (applied), **50%** product improvement (development). First regular three-category series. Becomes the norm.

**NSF nationalizes Anthony:**
- Extends to industry, government, university. Standards take ~a decade: government development merged with applied until 1957; industrial development not presented until 1959 (similar abroad, e.g. British DSIR 1958).
- NSF defs (aims/objectives, not output):
  - **Basic/fundamental:** original investigation for scientific knowledge; no specific commercial objectives (industry surveys add: may be in fields of present/potential company interest)
  - **Applied:** investigation for new scientific knowledge with specific commercial objectives (products or processes)
  - **Development:** nonroutine technical activity translating research findings or general scientific knowledge into products or processes
- Same statistical picture: development dominates. Government >60%; industry **76.9%**; nation **9.1% basic / 22.6% applied / 68.3% development**.

**From categories to a process model:**
- Anthony: a **spectrum** — basic at one end, development next to production/sale at the other.
- NSF (1952): “the technological sequence consists of basic research, applied research, and development,” and “each of the successive stages depends upon the preceding.”

## Economists appropriate the model (add innovation / diffusion)

- By early 1960s most countries share similar defs. Research = **R&D**, three activities.
- OECD standardizes. Frascati 1963: R&D surveys, indicators, policy targets such as **GERD/GDP**. Separate research from related scientific activities (scientific information, training, data collection, testing, standardization) and nonresearch (patent legal/admin, routine testing, technical services); separate development from production.
- Frascati three (1962, 12):
  - **Fundamental:** advancement of scientific knowledge, *without* a specific practical application in view
  - **Applied:** same, *with* a specific practical aim
  - **Development:** use of fundamental + applied results to introduce useful materials/devices/products/systems/processes or improve existing ones

**Economists arrive late.** Early 1960s they still debate whether development is “inventive” enough to count (Kuznets, Schmookler). Then they adopt the conventional taxonomy. Nelson 1962: NSF focused economists on R&D (“organized inventive activity”) and gave them numbers to work with.

**The extra dimension — innovation (commercial production):**
- People cite **Schumpeter** (1912/1934; 1939): invention / (initial) innovation / (innovation by) imitation or diffusion.
  - Invention = intellectual creativity, “without importance to economic analysis”
  - Innovation and diffusion = economic decisions, “closeness to economic use”: first application or first adoption
- Schumpeter himself: “Innovation is possible without anything we should identify as invention and invention does not necessarily induce innovation.” Sequential Schumpeter is an *interpreters’* product, especially in the technology-push / demand-pull debate.

**First sequential economic readings (1950s):**
- **Brozen (1951a):** Schumpeter’s three. **(1951b):** research, engineering development, production, service — “to capitalize on the discoveries of science.”
- **Maclaurin (MIT):** secretary of Bush’s Science and Public Welfare committee. 1953: Schumpeter made innovation central but “did not devote much attention to the role of science.” Five measurable steps: **pure science, invention, innovation, finance, acceptance/diffusion.** Earlier (1949 radio book): fundamental / applied / engineering development / production engineering / service engineering.
- Carter & Williams (1957–58): circuit from pure scientist to consumer; no formal model until Williams 1967.
- 1960 NBER/SSRC conference: production-function / input-output, *not* an innovation-process model.
- **Ruttan (1959):** Usher’s invention steps + Schumpeter → **Invention → Innovation → Technological Change.**

**1960s models:**
- **Ames (1961):** “innovation has come to mean all things to all men”; still offers a “sequence of markets”: research, invention (applied research), development, innovation. Feeds Machlup’s knowledge-society measurement (1962).
- **Schmookler (1966):** research, development, inventive activity — really about *invention* and demand/wants, not innovation.
- **Scherer (1965, Watt–Boulton):** invention, entrepreneurship, investment, development.
- **Mansfield (1968):** invention / innovation (first application) / diffusion (first use).

**Management schools popularize:**
- Myers & Marquis (1969, for NSF): recognition (technical feasibility *and* demand) → idea formulation → problem solving → solution → utilization and diffusion.
- Utterback (1974): generation of an idea → problem-solving/development → implementation and diffusion.

These efforts add diffusion to the much-quoted model: **Basic → Applied → Development → (Production and) Diffusion.**

**Two extra sources of “diffusion”:**
1. **Sociology of diffusion.** Ogburn & Gilfillan, NRC 1937 technology report: first description of innovation as a *social* process; diffusion plus social impacts as an ultimate phase. Note 19: NRC Subcommittee (Ogburn) — beginnings, development, diffusion, social influences. 1933 social-trends report: idea → trial device → demonstration → regular use → adoption.
   - **Rogers, *Diffusion of Innovations* (1962):** innovation, communication/diffusion, consequences on the social system, consequences through time. 3rd ed. (1983) assimilates the economic view: needs/problems → research → development → commercialization → diffusion and adoption → consequences.
2. **Product life cycle:** S-curve. Three phases: innovation (product), maturation (process), standardization (Vernon 1966; Utterback & Abernathy 1975).

**By early 1960s the sequence is “conventional” (Little/NSF 1963) and a management “lesson” (Bright 1969).**
- **Invention** = new idea for a product/process + reduction to practice. (Shortcut for Basic → Applied → Development.)
- **Innovation** = bringing invention into commercial use, or an invention so brought.
- **Diffusion** = spread of innovation in industry.

**Hindsight vs. TRACES (the first big fight):**
- DoD pioneered R&D categories and used the linear model to manage programs (Lazure 1957). Mid-1960s it defects from optimism about basic research as a factor in economic development. **Project Hindsight** (1969): 8 years, 20 major weapon technologies → only **0.3%** of innovation events from “undirected science.”
- NSF replies: **TRACES** (IIT 1968) + Battelle (1973) — **70%** of key events in five recent technological innovations stemmed from basic research.
- Each study carries its community’s message (industrialists/defense vs. scientists/NSF). First of a long series of criticisms of the linear model.

**Table 2 — taxonomies of innovation (selected):**
- Mees 1920: pure science, development, manufacturing
- Schumpeter 1939: invention, innovation, imitation
- Stevens 1941: fundamental → … → production/improvement/trouble shooting/technical control
- Bichowsky 1942: research, engineering/development, factory/production
- Furnas 1948: exploratory+fundamental, applied, development, production
- Mees & Leermakers 1950: research, development (small-scale / pilot / manufacturing)
- Brozen 1951a/b: invention-innovation-imitation **or** research-engineering development-production-service
- Maclaurin 1953: pure science, invention, innovation, finance, acceptance
- Ruttan 1959: invention, innovation, technological change
- Ames 1961: research, invention, development, innovation
- Scherer 1965: invention, entrepreneurship, investment, development
- Schmookler 1966: research, development, invention
- Mansfield 1968: invention, diffusion, innovation
- Myers & Marquis 1969: problem solving, solution, utilization, diffusion
- Utterback 1974: idea, problem-solving/development, implementation and diffusion

## Conclusion

Not Bush’s spontaneous invention. **Three steps / three communities / three policy priorities:**

| Step | Who | Stamp added | Policy preoccupation |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Natural scientists (academic + industrial) | applied research / technology, from pure/basic | public support for university research (basic) |
| 2 | Business-school researchers (in S&T studies before economists) | experimental development | strategic importance of technology for industry |
| 3 | Economists from business schools | innovation; then production and diffusion | impact of research on economy and society |

Each community entered by adding its term to the primitive “pure/basic research” sequence.

**Why it persists despite 1960s criticisms of linearity** (Schmookler; Price & Bass; Myers & Marquis) — and despite documents that use it *and* call linearity a fiction:
1. **Simplicity.** A rhetorical entity; a thought figure that orients administrators on R&D funding.
2. **More important: official statistics.** Three components presented one after another in a linear framework from the 1950s. For a long time (and still, for many) the *only* stats on the internal organization of research, especially in firms. Once innovation defined the science-policy agenda, R&D numbers became a legitimate **proxy** for technological innovation because they included development of new products and processes. Entrenched by statistics + methodological rules → **social fact**.

**Why alternatives fail:**
- Feedback-loop models (Kline 1985; Rothwell 1992) look like modern artwork or a **“plate of spaghetti and meatballs”** (Kelly et al. 1975, contrasting their “ecological” model).
- Official measurement of interactive models is unfinished (flows of knowledge across sectors, performers/users, activity types).
- Official innovation surveys have not produced cost-of-innovation numbers robust enough to supplement R&D figures.

**Last line:** statistics are often required to give (long) life to concepts; their absence limits changing analytical models and frameworks.
