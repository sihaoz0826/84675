# Science, Technology and International Relations — Fast Read

**Author:** Charles Weiss | *Technology in Society*, 2005

**What this is:** A framework article that sorts how science and technology hit world politics.

**Thesis:** Science, technology, and international affairs shape each other so much that IR should treat this as its own subfield. Keeping it as a specialist side topic is a dangerous leftover from the past.

## Main points

- **Science** here means knowledge from studying the natural world. **Technology** means putting technical knowledge to work — hardware, software, and some management, not just gadgets or IT.
- They work *with* other forces. They do not explain everything by themselves. Politics also shapes funding, who can research, and how tech spreads.
- Weiss sorts the effects into four channels.
- **How the system is built** (architecture): who is powerful, what “security” means, and how states relate. Nuclear weapons changed security. New tech can reorder military and economic power and create new problems, resources, and coalitions.
- **How the system runs** (processes): diplomacy, war, trade, finance, intelligence. Tech forces decisions at “Internet speed,” shifts who has the upper hand, and blurs old lines (soldier vs. civilian in cyber conflict). This is the clearest hit from information technology.
- **What policymakers face** (substance): new issues, new limits, and problems that are both domestic and international. Those limits include the laws of nature, not just politics. Climate change is his main example.
- **What people see and think** (perceptions and ideas): tech changes how power looks, adds information and openness, and gives IR theory new concepts. Sputnik changed the *look* of power, not only the hardware.
- He tests the four channels on the information and communications revolution. It touches almost every part of IR. The Internet also gives power to individuals and NGOs.
- Because tech hits both the facts of power and the *image* of power, the same framework works whether you treat power as real or as something people invent. He offers it as a bridge between IR schools, not a new school.
- In practice, science and technology are still sidelined: thin in IR courses, missing from the U.S. Foreign Service exam. That isolation is the leftover he wants to kill.

## Lines worth remembering

- Isolating science and technology from mainstream IR is a dangerous leftover.
- Four hits: how the system is built, how it runs, what issues it faces, and what people perceive.
- Foreign-policy limits include the laws of nature, not just politics.
- Sputnik: a tech stunt can change the *look* of power.
- Policymakers now must act at Internet speed.
