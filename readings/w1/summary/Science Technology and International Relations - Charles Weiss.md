# Science, Technology and International Relations — Fast Read (all recovered main points)

**Author:** Charles Weiss | **Technology in Society** 27(3), 2005, pp. 295–313
**What this is:** A typology article. Weiss argues S&T and international affairs *co-evolve*, and that treating S&T as a specialist side topic in IR is a dangerous anachronism. He offers a four-mechanism classification of how science and technology hit the international system, then tests it on the information/communications revolution.

**Core claim / thesis.** Science, technology, and international affairs affect one another so pervasively — especially via information and communication technologies — that the field should be an independent IR sub-discipline. Keeping it esoteric and isolated from mainstream IR is a dangerous anachronism.

## Definitions (how he uses the words)

- **Science** (natural science): “knowledge obtained by the systematic study of the structure and behavior of the natural world.”
- **Technology:** “the practical application of technical knowledge.” Includes hardware, software, and some aspects of management. Broader than everyday usage. Includes, but is much broader than, information technology.

S&T act *along with other factors*; they are not a master variable that explains everything by themselves.

---

## The four mechanisms (the framework to remember)

Impacts of S&T on international affairs operate through **four** channels:

1. **Architecture** of the international system — structure, key organizing concepts, and relations among actors.
2. **Processes** by which the system operates — diplomacy, war, administration, policy formation, commerce, trade, finance, communications, intelligence.
3. **Substance / operational environment of foreign policy** — new issue areas, new constraints and trade-offs. “Operational environment” includes not only political constraints but also constraints imposed by the **laws of natural and social science**.
4. **Perceptions, information, and ideas** — changed perceptions; information and transparency for how the system operates; new concepts and ideas for IR theory.

He also orders the discussion as four *aspects of the system*: operational processes, substance, architecture, and the information/ideas/perceptions it runs on.

---

## 1. Processes (clearest, most direct hit — especially from IT)

Operational processes include:

- **Mostly governments:** diplomacy, war, administration, policy formation, crisis management, intelligence gathering.
- **Mostly private sector:** commerce, trade, economic competition, finance, communications, and — most directly for S&T — the management and financing of research and innovation.

What technology does to processes:
- Forces policymakers to act at **“Internet speed.”**
- Changes relationships between actors: aggressor/defender, regulator/regulated, buyer/seller.
- Blurs old categories: e.g. combatant vs. non-combatant in cyber conflict.

**ICT test case:** the information, communications, and remote-sensing revolution hits virtually every aspect of IR and is his test of the classification. The *clearest and most direct* ICT impact is on operational processes. The Internet has empowered individuals and NGOs.

---

## 2. Substance (new issues, constraints, “intermestic” problems, IR theory)

S&T change the *content* of international affairs by:

1. Creating **new issue areas**.
2. Creating **new constraints and trade-offs** in the operational environment of foreign policy.
3. Creating **“intermestic”** issues — the line between international and domestic blurs.
4. Changing the **scope and domain of different IR-theory paradigms**.

**Operational environment** = the (often unperceived) external context that defines the universe of options a decision-maker actually has.

**Quintessential new constraint:** environmental damage from climate change — a physical/scientific constraint, not just a political one.

---

## 3. Architecture (structure, concepts, relations among actors)

Architecture = (1) structure, (2) key organizing concepts, (3) relations among constituent states and other actors.

Examples:
- **Structure:** bipolarity → monopolarity after the Soviet collapse; possible later shifts as technology-driven rise of other powers.
- **Organizing concepts:** nuclear weapons changed what “security” means and how states can achieve it. S&T also hit power, sovereignty, and national interest.

**Nine sub-lenses for how tech changes relations among actors (p. 303):**
1. Reordering hierarchies of **military** power
2. Reordering hierarchies of **economic** power
3. Redefining international **economic relations**
4. Creating or resolving international **problems**
5. Creating new **resources**
6. Creating new **coalitions**
7. Creating new **tools for international collaboration**
8. Creating new **arenas for cooperation and competition**
9. Professional **cooperation and communication among scientists** across countries also influences relations among nations

---

## 4. Perceptions, information, transparency, and IR-theory ideas

S&T:
- Alter perceptions (including of power).
- Supply information and transparency for operating the international system.
- Supply new concepts and metaphors for IR theory.

Example he uses: S&T affect not only the *objective* reality of military/economic power (divisions and equipment) but also the *perception* of power — via mass media and technological stunts like **Sputnik**.

---

## Mutual influence (it is not a one-way street)

The article’s title topic is co-evolution: IR also shapes science and technology, not only the reverse.

Recovered two-way list (Weiss 2005, pp. 297–299, as cited from that section):

**Technology → international relations**
1. Creates new artefacts that directly hit world politics (classic: nuclear weapons change how a balance of power is constructed).
2. **Diffusion** speed of a new technology changes the distribution of power.
3. Tech is itself an arena of interstate **competition** (e.g. arms races).
4. Uneven **capacity to manage technology** differentiates states’ economic positions.

**International relations → technology**
1. Public opinion toward technologies affects **funding**.
2. Foreign-policy goals translate into which **research programs get funded**.
3. IR affects **scientist migration** and freedom to research.
4. **IP agreements** and international-law norms shape tech.

## Bridge across IR paradigms

Because S&T hit both the *objective* and the *perceived* versions of power, sovereignty, and national interest, the typology works whether those concepts are treated as real (realism) or as human constructs (constructivism). That is why he presents the classification as a **bridge between competing IR paradigms**, not as a fifth school.

---

## The policy/teaching punchline: “mainstream” S&T

Isolation of S&T from mainstream IR is “a curious anomaly in the 21st century.”

Evidence he cites:
- S&T are peripheral in most undergrad and graduate IR curricula.
- S&T are **absent from the U.S. Foreign Service entrance exam**.

Given how much this matters for **global governance**, that isolation is the “dangerous anachronism” of the thesis.

---

## Lines worth remembering

- Treating S&T as a specialist side topic in IR is a **dangerous anachronism**.
- Four hits: **architecture, processes, substance/constraints, perceptions/ideas**.
- Technology includes hardware, software, *and some management* — not just gadgets or IT.
- Foreign-policy “operational environment” includes **laws of natural and social science**, not just politics.
- Climate change is the model of a scientific constraint on foreign policy.
- Policymakers now must act at **Internet speed**.
- Sputnik: tech stunts change the *perception* of power, not only the inventory of power.
- S&T can bridge realism and constructivism because they hit both facts and perceptions.
- S&T absent from the Foreign Service exam = the mainstreaming failure in one line.
