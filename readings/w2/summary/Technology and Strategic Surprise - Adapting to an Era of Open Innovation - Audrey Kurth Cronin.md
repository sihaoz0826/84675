# Technology and Strategic Surprise — Audrey Kurth Cronin (fast read)

**What this is:** A 2020 *Parameters* article (US Army War College) on how the US military should think about technology now that most innovation is commercial and open to almost anyone.

**Thesis:** The digital age is an *open* tech era like the late 1800s (tinkerers inventing radio, planes, and high explosives), not the 20th-century *closed* era of secret government programs—and the military will keep getting strategically surprised if it keeps using that old playbook.

## Main points

- Tech revolutions that change military power are either **closed** (expensive, secret, government-run) or **open** (cheap, commercial, lots of people can use and remix them).
- The 20th century was mainly closed: nukes, jets, radar. Elites locked them down. Change meant upgrading known systems. The digital age is open: phones, drones, 3D printing, AI. Companies chase users and markets, not military contracts.
- Starting in 1993, the US chose to open government-built IT (internet, GPS, chips) to global commercial development. Rivals got roughly the same access. Tech did not actually speed up—it only feels faster because tools now mix, spread worldwide, and touch more of daily life, including fighting.
- The military cannot invent its way out of this. It has to work with commercial innovation, not try to own it.
- Even in closed eras, how people used tech often mattered more than who had the fancier gadget (Britain used radar better than Germany; many US submarine skippers had the wrong habits).
- The best historical match is the late 1800s. Nobel (dynamite), Marconi (radio), and the Wright brothers (planes) were cheap backyard inventors. Those tools later reshaped war—and dynamite plus the telegraph also enabled a global wave of terrorist bombings.
- Same pattern now: cheap facial recognition, social media, robots, and everyday connected devices give private groups and individuals tools that used to need an army. The next “big thing” in war may be many little things used in new ways.
- The Pentagon’s “Third Offset” (military robots and human–machine teams) uses the wrong model. Cold War offsets used US-controlled tech. Today the surprises come from commercial stuff not built for the military.
- To stay ahead: treat military innovation as commercially driven; think about all of society, not just state-vs-state fights; and let people move in and out of service without career punishment. Do not copy China or Russia by forcing companies to serve the military.
- Open-innovation periods are exciting and unstable. Surprise is now inevitable—plan for it. Inspire today’s tinkerers instead of trying to control them.

## Lines worth remembering

- The right analogue is the late 19th century, not the nuclear age.
- The military cannot innovate its way out of an open revolution.
- The next big thing in war may be many little things used by ordinary people.
- Nuclear deterrence still matters for nukes and is not enough for everything else.
- Technological surprise is now inevitable; build it into planning.
- Work with tinkerers; don’t try to own innovation.
