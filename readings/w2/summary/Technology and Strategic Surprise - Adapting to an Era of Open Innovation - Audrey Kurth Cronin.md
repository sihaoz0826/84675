# Technology and Strategic Surprise: Adapting to an Era of Open Innovation — Audrey Kurth Cronin (Parameters, 2020)

**Thesis.** The digital revolution is an *open* technological era. The right historical analogue is the late 19th century (tinkerers inventing radio, airplanes, high explosives), not the 20th-century closed era of state-run nuclear/radar/jet programs. US strategists will get strategically surprised if they keep using 20th-century closed-innovation frameworks. The military cannot innovate its way out of this; it must work with, draw from, and adapt to commercially driven open innovation.

---

## Opening argument

- The US rose to global power in a *closed* tech context. Today’s digital revolution is *open*. 20th-century strategy tools will not prevent surprise.
- Starting in 1993, the US deliberately opened maturing IT to global commercial development. Competitors and adversaries got roughly the same access as the US and its allies.
- Tech did not actually get faster; it only *felt* faster because technologies now interact, globally diffuse, and hit more of human life (including conflict).
- That commercial IT boom happened *outside* the US military.
- Success in warfare now is not direct military tech development. The military cannot out-innovate an open revolution. It must work with it, draw from it, and adapt.

---

## Open vs. closed technological revolutions

Tech revolutions that affect military innovation and state power are either open or closed.

**Closed (mainly 20th century)**
- Nuclear weapons, battleships, jets, radar: expensive, rare, hard to build, long-term government programs.
- Needs specialized expertise. Elites restrict access via secrecy, clearances, patents, copyrights.
- Governments track *proliferation* of high-end lethal tech and wall it off from the public and from each other.
- Change is incremental upgrades on known systems (PGMs, warhead size, stealth).
- Slow, complex, proprietary. Military sets requirements; contractors chase profits.
- Language is *dual-use* (military vs civilian).
- Costs climb as systems (F-22, Arleigh Burke, Trident II) are upgraded to cut risk, meet new standards, protect sunk costs, and keep a lead in known capabilities.

**Open (mainly 21st century)**
- Driven by commercial processes, not the military.
- Popular access to potentially lethal tech; it affects all of society.
- Users need not be scientists or even understand the tech. Platforms are cheap, user-friendly, built for experiment.
- Google, Facebook, Microsoft drive development; their goal is expanding global markets by pulling users in.
- Example: a smartphone is ~4x as powerful as NASA’s Curiosity rover computer, yet easy to use. Cheap software lets people livestream, send encrypted messages, steal data, or do facial recognition targeting. Same phone can guide a UAV or robot.
- Open eras encourage *tinkerers*.
- Replace *dual-use* with *multiuse*: professionals, pro-sumers, hobbyists, consumers all experiment.
- These techs *diffuse* (like telegraph, railroad, radio, auto), they do not *proliferate* (like nuke/chem/bio).
- High-end weapons problems remain, *plus* instability from lethal uses of democratized tech.
- Ordinary people buy, use, remix techs into new purposes, forms, and surprise combinations—often beyond inventors’ intent. Examples: Apple I/II tinkerers, student quadcopters, database hackers, 3D-printed guns, 2019 Hong Kong protesters mixing shortwave radios with smartphones.
- Resulting clusters: smartphones, UAVs, robotics, CRISPR, additive manufacturing, machine learning, simple AI—all widely accessible.
- Taken together, these clusters matter as much for future war as Hiroshima 1945.
- 20th-century nuclear-era strategies/theories do not fit this era.

## War and technology (what older arguments still do / don’t apply)

Dominant Western narrative for ~500 years: major powers concentrate ever more advanced, lethal systems under state control, ending in nuclear weapons.

- Brodie & Brodie, *From Crossbow to H-Bomb* (1962/1973): weaponry slow for most of history, sped up in the 19th c., faster in the 20th, peaked at Hiroshima/Nagasaki. Superpower nukes then “proved” state-controlled military tech was decisive.
- That *centralization* story has holes: 17th-c. Europe outsourced military power to private contractors; Brodies skipped cases where new tech was counterproductive, and periods of more distributed power (9th–10th c. Europe). 1973 edition, during Vietnam, worried the war had slowed tech development and noted rising weapons costs.
- Still, the view that military tech innovation *drives* the evolution of war dominated the 20th century. Van Creveld (1989): war is completely permeated and governed by technology.
- Focusing on states racing for a tech edge made sense then: in many 20th-c. wars, advanced tech did decide outcomes. WWII and interwar innovation studies mattered because *how* great powers developed and used military tech shaped results.

**Human factors still matter (even in closed eras)**
- Germans were most technically advanced at the start of WWII but failed to use radar well: interservice rivalry, Luftwaffe resistance (Udet: radar would “take all the fun out of flying”), dropped shorter-wavelength research, no good operational doctrine.
- Britain was behind on radar tech but wove it into every part of air defense in the Battle of Britain. Churchill: British achievement was *operational efficiency*, not novelty of equipment.
- US submarines: capable long-range boats, but peacetime training taught commanders to attack escorted warships, stay stealthy, use sonar, avoid exposure. Wartime mission became attacking fast Japanese merchant convoys, which required periscope depth. Rosen: only 31 of 4,873 known US sub attacks were sonar-directed. Commanders stayed invisible and missed targets until aggressive younger skippers took over. 30% of US sub commanders relieved for cause in 1942.
- Lesson from 20th-c. innovation studies: doctrine, training, and culture decide whether tech is used well. *Even so*, the prevailing view stayed: sophisticated military-controlled tech is the lynchpin of state advantage.

**Revolution in Military Affairs (RMA) as the end of the closed-era logic**
- Late-20th-c. RMA stayed in that tradition: high-end, large-scale, US-dominated info-age systems (PGMs, satellites, C2, networked ops) would nearly remove guesswork from war. Gulf War 1990–91 seemed to confirm it.
- Adm. William Owens: correctly applied tech for see/tell/act yields dominant battlespace knowledge, near-perfect mission assignment, immediate/complete battlespace assessment—battlefield as transparent and controllable.
- Paradox: this closed-era theory peaked *just as* the US government was opening key techs to commercial development and global diffusion.
- 1990s: military innovation practice diverged from commercial policy on government-developed tech. Gap worsened. ~30 years later, open innovation has matured and the US military is neither driving it nor keeping up.

## Pandora’s Box (how the US opened the system)

- Shift began **1993**, as deliberate post–Cold War US policy (Clinton/Gore high-tech growth agenda).
- 1960s–80s publicly financed, government-controlled R&D powered the 1990s boom. Then R&D money and tax incentives moved from defense to civilian industry.
- Government roots of today’s commercial stack: ARPANET → Internet; tax dollars → GPS; NSF grant helped Google’s search engine; smartphone guts (microchips, touchscreens, Siri-style voice) came from US government programs.
- Contrast: **Manhattan Project** (secret, state-run bomb) vs. **today’s ML/AI** (private companies: Microsoft, IBM, Facebook, Amazon, Apple, Alphabet).
- AI spend: ~$35.8B worldwide in 2019 (+44% vs 2018), expected to double by 2022.
- Pentagon stood up Joint AI Center, but commercial actors (e.g. Microsoft: compute, cloud, data for deep learning) have a **~10-year lead**.
- Tech firms fully globalized. Google’s 2017 Beijing AI institute: “the science of AI has no borders.”
- We are heading into automation, autonomy, narrow AI, maybe AGI.
- Most threat analysis still uses nuclear-era tools: *deterrence* and *compellence*. Nukes still matter; history still matters. But analysis must widen past formal militaries *and* look at earlier open eras.
- Punchline: the next “big thing” in warfare may be a bunch of *little* things used by ordinary people in new ways.

## Lessons from the 19th century (the real analogue)

Last comparable open period: second half of the 19th century, when globalized industrialization matured the way today’s information age is ripening.

- When innovation is open and change is rapid, *all of society* is permeated by tech, not just war.
- No sharp amateur/professional science line. Then: mail-order/hardware-store wiring kits, chemicals, explosives. Now: quadcopters, robots, gene-splicing kits, 3D-printed-gun files.
- 19th-c. social change was even broader than today’s (habitation, transport, energy, food, sanitation, medicine). Newly literate publics wanted in.
- Popular science magazines: *Popular Science Review* (1862), *Nature* (1869), *Scientific American* (1845), *Popular Science Monthly* (1872).

**Civilian tinkerers produced war-critical tech, cheaply, outside government**
- Nobel (1867): dynamite, from backyard nitroglycerin experiments. Also the *blasting cap* (mercury fulminate detonates nitroglycerine)—method later used from artillery to atom bombs. Also *ballistite* → a class of munitions and rapid-fire artillery.
- Marconi (1896): radio, homemade gear in a Bologna attic.
- Wright brothers (1903): powered flight from a Dayton bicycle workshop.
- High explosives, radio, airplanes: civilian, open innovation, **under $1,000 each**. Crucial to later war. None came from (or arguably could have come from) government programs.

**Open lethal tech also fueled nonstate violence**
- Dynamite enabled the first global modern terrorism wave (anarchist wave): every continent but Antarctica; thousands killed/injured; assassinations of presidents, PMs, monarchs from Russia through Europe to the US.
- Undersea telegraph spread graphic “dynamiting” news; fueled Pulitzer/Hearst mass press. Anarchist papers/pamphlets circulated worldwide. Dynamite was cheap (e.g. 36¢/lb in Oregon).
- By Franz Ferdinand’s assassination (28 June 1914): a **trifecta** was already in place—(1) openly accessible lethal tech, (2) new communications vectors, (3) diffused individual/small-group violence. That mix resembles today.

## Contemporary parallels

- Today’s IT innovation is driven as much by popular tinkering as by secret elite programs.
- Pre-WWI key was *not* just the 1897–1914 Anglo-German Dreadnought race. Today’s key is *not* just the US–China AI arms race.
- Pre-WWI bigger picture: great-power politics *plus* civilian telegraph, steel, machine tooling, coal→petroleum, stable high explosives. Those commercial innovations spawned industrial killing machines Europe was unprepared for. Belligerents learned by bloody trial and error in the war. Nobody had assessed the preexisting *open* tech context. Cost of learning on the job was cataclysmic.

**Same structure now**
- Future war’s story is happening *outside* the military.
- Commercial clusters changing how force is used: social media, additive manufacturing, robotics, driverless vehicles, IoT, ML, evolving AI.
- Political/psych: digital profiling + weaponized social media transform mobilization and PSYOP.
- Physical: cheap facial recognition democratizes assassination; IoT makes everyone attackable.
- Functions that long required a funded, trained army are now available to private actors and individuals—not at army-level competence, but good enough to kill and to have political impact.

**What the military should (and should not) do**
- Pay closer attention to accessible open techs, especially *who is using them*.
- Violence is changing in authoritarian states *and from below*, degrading US military effectiveness in both state and nonstate fights.
- **Third Offset Strategy** (military robotics, human-machine teaming) is well-funded and admirable but uses the *wrong analogy*. Cold War offsets used US-controlled tech (nukes, PGMs) against Soviet mass/geography. Today threats and opportunities come from surprise *commercial* advances not built for the military and not under state control.

**State adversaries still matter—but US policy helped level the field**
- Chinese tech espionage has badly hurt the US and allies. Work & Grant: China stole/exploited Western secrets and leveled the field with the Joint Force in some key capabilities in under two decades.
- Also true: 1990s US choices to privatize and share digital tech *made Internet-assisted economic espionage easy*. From a 1990s vantage, one person’s espionage is another’s open access.
- Question after opening Pandora’s box: how does the US military adapt and still come out ahead?

## How to succeed (three moves)

US still leads in key civilian/military techs (robotics, AI). Keeping that lead requires reversing the **1990s split between commercial policy and government innovation policy**.

1. **Reconceptualize** military tech innovation as commercially driven and open.
2. **Reorganize** around whole-of-society strategic concepts; drop neat state-on-state models. Tech companies are now richer and more powerful than many states.
3. **Personnel**: work with, educate, train, and reward people who can win Fourth Industrial Revolution wars, where surprise threats come as readily from small groups, individuals, or private firms as from major powers (who already use those actors as proxies).

**Strategic picture**
- Tech no longer backs the Western nation-state *centralization-of-force* story.
- Who *is* centralizing power now: tech companies, and authoritarians (China, Russia). COVID-19 surveillance further strengthens authoritarian states.
- Nukes still exist on both sides. They now sit alongside diffuse, widely accessible digital techs.
- Nuclear deterrence remains necessary for nuclear weapons and insufficient against the rest of 21st-c. tech.

**What conflict looks like**
- Professional armies blur into proxies; nonstate actors field unexpected lethal capabilities.
- Cyber contests, economic espionage, IoT hacking, intel theft sit below interstate war but accumulate as a real threat.
- US domestic polarization is an exploitable weakness.
- Democratized tech favors harassment, disruption, and attrition that erode strength.
- Smart regulation is a *national security* job, not only law enforcement: IoT security standards, resilience to online PSYOP, privacy, personal data rights, stop wholesale database hacking.

**Work with tinkerers, don’t try to own innovation**
- 19th-c. inventors (Wrights, Nobel, Marconi) did not want to join the military; they wanted to invent independently.
- When government *was* the research frontier, talent went to it (Oppenheimer, Teller, Fermi → Manhattan Project / Los Alamos). 20th-c. services and RAND drew top scientists because cutting-edge physics/engineering was government-funded.
- That is not true now.
- Too late to recapture cutting-edge digital innovation inside traditional military/government orgs at scale. “Military/defense innovation” is the wrong frame.
- Coercing companies to serve military aims (Russia/China model) is both impossible and undesirable.
- There is still time: innovation is actually slow. Advantage comes from appealing to innovators’ ideals and offsetting their economic risk. Most tech workers and entrepreneurs want to serve the public good.
- Companies (Microsoft, Google, Facebook) must remember history: brilliant inventions get used regardless of inventors’ intent. Microsoft’s 2019 $1B OpenAI bet (AGI); Amazon (e-commerce AI); Google (DeepMind). Without ethics, restraints, and historical literacy, *US commercial actors* can start an arms race humanity loses just as easily as the military can.

**Tear down the military–civilian firewall**
- Let unorthodox talent move in and out of service *without career penalty*. Officer promotion paths are anachronistic and don’t produce the needed experience mix.
- Example: if Army Futures Command is only lifelong civil servants and officers who never worked a startup, built cutting-edge tech, or took entrepreneurial risk (including failure), it will not work with the tech sector, spot promising commercial techs, write forward doctrine, or use evolving capabilities in contests.

**The Silicon Valley rift is economic, not mainly cultural**
- Google 2018 Project Maven protests notwithstanding: culture is not the core problem.
- Reagan Institute 2018: 53% under 29 have “great confidence” in the military; more Americans trust military officers (59%) than doctors (54%), teachers (52%), clergy (25%). Youth still trust the military as an institution.
- What innovators fear is industrial-era bureaucracy. Furloughs of skilled government professionals make it worse.
- DoD contracting is hard, risky, expensive, slow, and biased toward incumbents who know the system. Most startups cannot survive it. Commercial markets scale new ideas faster.

---

## Closing

Open-innovation periods are exciting *and* socially unstable. Militaries will keep having to respond.

- Do not only ask how new tech might be used on the battlefield. Tech hits societies in *uncontested* environments first.
- Miss the broader social/political/economic context of public *and* private innovation → unprepared for how the next war actually unfolds.
- Wrong innovation models (closed, 20th-century) → outmoded strategy and doctrine.
- **Technological surprise is now inevitable; build it into US planning.**
- Do not try to wrest control of chaotic open innovation. Inspire and incentivize today’s “whiz kids”—the Nobels, Marconis, and Wrights of this century—to channel creativity toward American interests.
